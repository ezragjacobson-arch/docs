## Know Your Local Laws

Self-defence law is not uniform. The legal frameworks governing weapons, use of force, and self-defence claims differ dramatically across jurisdictions. Review each of the areas below and research the specific rules that apply to you.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Firearm Ownership & Licensing">
    Firearm ownership requirements vary widely — some jurisdictions require background checks, waiting periods, safety courses, or licensing before you may legally possess a firearm. Others impose restrictions based on firearm type, magazine capacity, or calibre. Research your national, state or provincial, and local regulations before purchasing or possessing any firearm, and renew licences and registrations as required by law.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Concealed Carry & Open Carry">
    The right to carry a firearm in public — whether concealed or openly — is governed by separate legislation from the right to own one. Many jurisdictions require a specific permit or licence to carry, restrict carry in designated locations such as schools, government buildings, and places of worship, or prohibit carry entirely. Reciprocity agreements between states or provinces are not universal, so carrying across borders requires independent legal verification.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Knife Laws">
    Knife laws vary enormously by jurisdiction and often by knife type. Blade length limits, locking mechanisms, assisted-opening mechanisms, and fixed-blade restrictions are all commonly regulated. Some jurisdictions distinguish between possession in the home and carrying in public, and many impose stricter rules on carrying in specific locations. Research the knife laws in every jurisdiction you travel to, not just where you reside.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Use of Force and Self-Defence Laws">
    The legal standard for justified use of force differs between jurisdictions. Most require that force be proportionate to the threat faced and that a genuine, reasonable belief in imminent harm existed at the time. Some jurisdictions impose a legal duty to retreat before using force if it is safe to do so, while others do not. Understanding the precise standard in your jurisdiction is essential — misjudging it can turn a defensive act into a criminal one.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Castle Doctrine vs. Duty to Retreat">
    Castle Doctrine laws, where they exist, generally permit residents to use force — including lethal force — to defend their home without a duty to retreat first. Stand Your Ground laws extend a similar principle to public spaces in some jurisdictions. Duty to Retreat jurisdictions, by contrast, require that you attempt to safely withdraw from a confrontation before resorting to force, where withdrawal is reasonably possible. These frameworks are not universal: confirm which applies in your jurisdiction and understand its precise legal requirements.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
