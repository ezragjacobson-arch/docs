Taking someone down is only half the equation. Once you reach the ground, you need to control the opponent to prevent them from continuing the attack, accessing a weapon, or creating enough space to escape and re-engage you from a standing position. Ground control is the systematic language of position — and position determines everything that follows.

## The Ground Control Hierarchy

Brazilian jiu-jitsu and wrestling have mapped positional dominance on the ground with considerable precision. Not all ground positions are equal: some give you complete control and offensive options; others leave you exposed. The general hierarchy, from most to least dominant, runs: back control, mount, knee-on-belly, side control, and guard (where you are on your back with the opponent between your legs).

Each step down the hierarchy reduces your control and increases the opponent's ability to escape, strike, or reverse the position. Your goal in any ground engagement is to advance up the hierarchy — or, in a self-defence context, to reach a stable control position and disengage.

<Note>
  In a competitive grappling context, maintaining ground control and working for a submission is the goal. In a real-world self-defence scenario, your goal is different: establish enough control to neutralise the immediate threat, then disengage and create distance. Staying on the ground unnecessarily — even in a dominant position — introduces new risks, including other attackers, weapons, and hard surfaces. Control with a purpose; exit when you can.
</Note>

## Mount Position

Mount is the position where you sit astride the opponent's torso, your knees on either side of their hips or ribcage. It is one of the most dominant positions in ground fighting — your weight pins them, your hands are free, and their striking ability is severely limited.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Establish the Position">
    Sit squarely on the opponent's hips or lower abdomen, with your knees pinched tight against their sides. Do not sit high on the chest — sitting on the hips gives you better weight distribution and makes it harder for them to bridge.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Control the Hands and Wrists">
    Grab the opponent's wrists and pin them to the mat near their head, or use your hands to post against their shoulders and chest. Controlling the wrists prevents them from grabbing your collar or pushing your hips to create an escape.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Keep Your Hips Low">
    Resist the urge to sit upright and raise your hips. A low hip position makes you much heavier and far harder to bridge off. Think of melting your weight down into them rather than sitting on top of them.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Deliver Strikes if Necessary">
    From mount, downward hammer fists, forearm strikes, and elbows are effective if continued force is warranted. Keep one hand posting for balance if you are striking with the other.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Maintain Awareness of Escape Attempts">
    The two most common escapes from mount are the bridge-and-roll (the bottom person arches their hips and rolls you) and the elbow-knee escape (they make space and insert a knee to recover guard). Recognise these attempts early: widen your base when you feel a bridge coming and keep your knees tight when they try to hip escape.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Side Control

Side control (cross-body) places you beside the opponent rather than on top, with your chest pressed across their chest and torso. It is slightly less dominant than mount but easier to establish directly from a takedown and more stable against certain escapes.

To establish side control, drive your chest down across theirs with your hip pressing into their nearside hip. Use a nearside underhook (your arm under their armpit) to control the shoulder, and either wrap their head with your far arm or post your forearm against the far side of their jaw. The combination of chest pressure, hip pressure, and shoulder control makes it very difficult for them to roll.

Knee-on-belly is a variant where you place one knee across the opponent's abdomen, keeping the other foot posted on the mat. It applies significant pressure and is highly mobile — you can transition to mount, the far side, or stand up quickly. However, it is less stable than full side control and experienced grapplers can push the knee off. Use it as a transitional position or when you need to be ready to stand quickly.

## Back Control

Back control — where you are behind the opponent — is the highest-control position in ground grappling. It gives you access to the most powerful restraint holds and chokes while the opponent has almost no effective offensive options.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Achieve the Rear Position">
    Transition to back control from a scramble, a failed mount escape, or directly from a rear takedown entry. You need to be behind the opponent with your chest against their back.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Insert the Hooks">
    Slide your feet inside the opponent's thighs, hooking inward. These "hooks" — your feet anchoring against the inside of their legs — prevent them from rolling sideways and keep them tethered to you. Do not cross your feet in front.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply the Seat Belt Grip">
    Wrap one arm over the opponent's shoulder and across their chest (the "over" arm), and the other arm under the opposite armpit (the "under" arm). Clasp your hands together in the middle of their chest. This grip gives you control of their entire upper body.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Issue Commands or Transition to Restraint">
    From back control with hooks, the opponent has very limited options. Use this position to give clear verbal commands ("Stop moving, I have you controlled"), apply a restraint hold, or transition to the rear naked choke if necessary to protect yourself.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
  Back control gives you direct access to the opponent's throat and neck. Any choke or strangling hold applied from this position — intentionally or accidentally — can cause loss of consciousness in seconds. Understand exactly what you are applying and why before training these controls. The rear naked choke is covered in detail in the Restraint and Compliance section.
</Warning>

## Guard Position

Guard is the position where you are on your back with the opponent between your legs. In Brazilian jiu-jitsu, guard is an active offensive position. In a real-world self-defence scenario, it is best understood as an emergency defensive position — a place you may find yourself through no choice of your own.

Closed guard (legs wrapped and locked around the opponent's waist) limits their ability to posture up and strike. Open guard (legs framing against their hips and shoulders) creates space and angles to sweep or stand. Neither is a place you want to stay in a self-defence context: the ground is hard, there may be other threats, and your mobility is severely compromised.

Your objectives from guard are clear and prioritised: sweep the opponent to take top position, apply a submission to end their ability to fight, or — most importantly — stand up and disengage. Guard should be a transit point, not a destination.

## Striking from Top Position

From mount and side control, you have the ability to deliver strikes downward — hammer fists, forearm strikes, elbows, and in close, short punches. Ground-and-pound can rapidly end an opponent's ability or willingness to continue the fight. Drive strikes from the shoulder and hip rather than just the arm; your body weight behind each strike is what makes them effective from this angle.

<Warning>
  Once an opponent on the ground has stopped actively resisting, continued striking may cross the threshold from self-defence into assault — regardless of what they did initially. The law does not permit unlimited force: your response must be proportionate to the ongoing threat. The moment a person is no longer capable of harming you, additional strikes create serious legal exposure for you. Stop when the threat stops. This is both a legal requirement and an ethical one.
</Warning>

## Disengaging from the Ground

In most self-defence contexts, achieving a ground control position is not the end goal — it is a platform from which you stabilise the situation and then stand up and reassess. Standing up safely from the ground requires care, especially if the opponent is partially conscious or there are other threats nearby.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Establish a Stable Base Before Moving">
    Before you attempt to stand, make sure you have a stable, controlled position on the ground. Do not rush the stand-up from a scrambled or compromised position — you are vulnerable mid-transition.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Watch for Counter-Attacks">
    As you shift your weight to disengage, watch the opponent's hands and hips. A partially downed person can grab a leg, create a trip, or reach for an object. Keep your eyes on them as you move.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Post and Push">
    Post one hand on the ground for support, push off the opponent or the mat to create distance, and use that push to drive your body backward and upward.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Create Distance Before Fully Standing">
    Do not stand straight up while still in contact range. Scramble backward on your hands and feet first — a "technical stand-up" — putting space between you and the opponent before your legs are fully under you.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Stand with Hands Raised">
    Come to your feet in a fighting stance with your hands raised — both to protect yourself if the opponent recovers faster than expected, and to signal to any witnesses or responding officers that you are not the aggressor.
  </Step>
</Steps>
