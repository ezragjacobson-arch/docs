Standing takedowns are techniques that bring an opponent from their feet to the ground, removing their ability to generate powerful strikes and giving you a decisive positional advantage. Executed correctly, they are among the most reliable tools for ending a standing confrontation — but like any high-commitment technique, they require timing, setup, and a clean finish to be effective.

## The Setup Phase

You cannot simply "shoot" for a takedown against a resisting opponent and expect it to work. An uncovered shot leaves your head exposed to uppercuts, guillotines, and knee strikes. Every successful takedown begins with a setup — a distraction, a jab to draw the hands high, a push to create a reaction, or a clinch entry that closes the distance safely. The shot itself is the second movement; the setup is the first.

<Tip>
  Time your takedown entry to the moment an opponent's weight is committed — either forward into a punch or back in a defensive reaction. A moving target off-balance is far easier to take down than a planted, centred opponent. Watch the feet: the moment a rear foot steps forward to drive a punch, that is your window.
</Tip>

## The Double Leg Takedown

The double leg is the highest-percentage takedown in wrestling and mixed martial arts. It targets both legs simultaneously, giving you control of the opponent's entire lower base.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Level Change">
    Drop your level by bending your knees deeply — not by folding at the waist. Keep your spine upright and your eyes on the target. Bending at the waist puts your head down and your neck at risk; bending at the knees keeps you explosive and protected.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Penetration Step">
    Drive your lead foot forward, planting it between the opponent's feet. Your lead knee should almost touch the ground. This step closes the distance explosively and gets your hips inside the opponent's hips — the critical alignment for the throw.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Head Position">
    As you shoot, drive your head to the outside of the opponent's near hip — not into their chest. Outside head position eliminates the most dangerous counter: the guillotine choke. If your head is inside (between their torso and arm), they can wrap your neck and defend the takedown simultaneously.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Secure Both Legs">
    Drive both arms around the backs of the opponent's thighs, locking your hands together. Your grip should be tight against the back of the knees, not around the ankles. This prevents them from sprawling away and losing the connection.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Drive Forward and Upward">
    With your legs driving and your hips extending, push through the opponent. Drive your shoulder into their hips and lift. The lifting and forward drive combined breaks their base and sends them backward and down.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Finish and Establish Top Position">
    Follow them to the mat, staying tight to the body. Do not let go at the moment of impact — use the momentum of the fall to land in top position, between their legs or moving immediately to side control. Your weight should be on them before they can react.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Note>
  Head position on the double leg is not a minor detail — it is the difference between landing in a dominant position and being choked unconscious mid-takedown. Default to outside head position (head outside the opponent's near hip) until you have enough experience to manage the inside position safely.
</Note>

## The Single Leg Takedown

The single leg targets one leg rather than two, making it easier to enter against opponents who protect their hips well. It is slightly lower-amplitude but offers more finishing options.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Set Up with a Strike or Feint">
    Use a jab, a rear-hand feint, or a push to the face to draw the opponent's hands upward and shift their attention. The single leg entry is shorter than the double, but you still need their weight to be moving or their guard to be open.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Level Change and Shoot to One Leg">
    Drop your level and drive your lead knee toward the ground, shooting to the outside of one of the opponent's legs. Secure the leg by wrapping both arms around it — one hand behind the knee, one around the shin or ankle.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Control the Leg and Head Position">
    You can carry the leg with your head on the inside (between the leg and their body) or on the outside. Inside head position allows for a more direct drive; outside head position gives you more safety against the opponent posting and sprawling over your back.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Choose Your Finish">
    There are two primary finishes. For the outside trip (sweep): step to the outside of the trapped leg, sweep it across your body and drive the opponent to the mat. For the "run the pipe": stand upright, lift the leg, and drive forward while pivoting their base out from under them.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Land on Top">
    Regardless of finish, drive through and land in a dominant top position — between their legs, in side control, or moving to mount. Use your grip on the leg to steer where they fall.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Note>
  The single leg has more finishing options than any other takedown. If the outside trip is blocked, transition to the run-the-pipe or the inside trip. Fluency between finishes — not commitment to one — is what makes the single leg reliable under pressure. Train all three finishes so you can shift without pausing.
</Note>

## Hip Throw (O-goshi / Seoi-nage)

Hip throws from judo use the opponent's upper body momentum and your hip as a fulcrum. They work at clinch range and require less leg drive than wrestling takedowns, making them effective against larger opponents when applied correctly.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Clinch Entry">
    Establish collar-and-elbow control: one hand gripping the opponent's collar or back of the neck, the other controlling the elbow. This grip gives you the leverage to control their posture and set up the throw.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Step Across the Body">
    Pivot and step your lead foot across to the outside of the opponent's lead foot, then bring your rear foot in so that your back is now against their chest. Both of your feet should be inside their stance, your hips aligned across and below theirs.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Load Onto Your Hip">
    Pull the opponent forward and upward with your collar grip while your hip — positioned across their hip — becomes the pivot point. Their upper body tips forward; their feet begin to leave the ground.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Bend Forward and Drive Through">
    Bend forward sharply at the waist, driving your grip toward the ground. This rotation finishes the arc of the throw, pulling the opponent over your hip and projecting them forward.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Follow to the Ground and Control">
    Maintain your grip through the throw — do not release. Step around to follow them to the mat and immediately establish side control or mount, preventing them from rolling to their feet.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Body Slam / Suplex

A body slam or rear suplex involves securing an underhook (arm under the opponent's armpit) or a rear waist lock, lifting, and driving them backward to the ground. In a rear suplex variation, you arch backward and throw the opponent over your head. The mechanics require upper-body strength, a tight clinch at the waist or chest, and a committed backward arch that transfers your momentum into theirs.

These are high-amplitude throws with correspondingly high risk. They are encountered in wrestling, freestyle grappling, and emergency close-quarters situations. They are not beginner techniques.

<Warning>
  Dropping a person onto the back of their head on a hard surface is potentially lethal. A rear suplex to a concrete floor can cause fatal head or spinal injuries. Body slam variations should only be trained under qualified supervision on appropriate matting. In a self-defence context, force must be proportionate to the threat: deploying a throw that is likely to kill or critically injure is justifiable only in the most extreme circumstances. Understand the legal and ethical weight of this before you train it.
</Warning>

## Defending Takedowns

Understanding takedown offence requires equal fluency in takedown defence. If you can be taken down easily, every opponent with a wrestling background has a decisive advantage over you.

<Accordion title="The Sprawl (Defending the Double Leg)">
  When an opponent shoots for your legs, the sprawl drives your hips backward and downward — away from their grip — while your upper body stays forward. As they reach for your legs, throw your hips back hard, drive your weight onto their upper back, and kick your legs back and out of their hands. The objective is to land chest-to-back on top of them with your hips on their lower back, taking away the clinch entirely.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Framing (Preventing Clinch Entry)">
  Before an opponent reaches clinch range, framing creates a physical barrier: a stiff arm to the forehead, shoulder, or chest stops forward progress. Framing is not a strike — it is a posting tool. Combined with lateral movement, it breaks the line of the shot and forces the opponent to reset.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="The Underhook Battle">
  In a clinch, the position of your arms determines who controls the exchange. Securing an underhook (your arm under their armpit, your hand on their back) gives you leverage for throws, trips, and takedown defence. The underhook battle — fighting to secure yours while denying theirs — is the strategic centre of the clinch game. Whichever fighter wins the underhook position typically controls what happens next.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Distance Management">
  Most takedown shots are launched from just outside arm's reach. Staying at striking range (where your hands and elbows are effective) or at long range (where a shot cannot bridge the gap before you react) reduces your exposure. The danger zone is the transitional middle distance — close enough for them to reach you, far enough that your arms cannot stop them. Manage distance actively; do not drift into the danger zone passively.
</Accordion>
