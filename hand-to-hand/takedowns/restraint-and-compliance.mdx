Restraint techniques allow you to control an individual without striking — keeping them immobilised and compliant until help arrives or until the threat has passed. Unlike strikes or takedowns that aim to end a confrontation through pain or incapacitation, restraint holds aim to manage it: holding a person in a controlled position with the minimum force necessary. These techniques are drawn directly from law enforcement and security training, where the ability to detain without injury is a professional requirement. Because the margin between effective control and serious injury is narrow, every technique in this section demands careful, supervised training before any real-world application.

<Warning>
  Restraint holds applied incorrectly, on the wrong part of the anatomy, or by undertrained individuals can cause serious injury or death. Joint locks can snap tendons and dislocate or fracture joints in a fraction of a second. Neck restraints applied to the trachea (windpipe) rather than the carotid arteries can cause crushing injury and death. Never apply any choke or hold around the front of the throat. These techniques must be trained under a qualified instructor in a controlled environment before you attempt them on another person — full stop.
</Warning>

## When to Restrain vs. When to Disengage

<Note>
  Restraint is a high-commitment action that keeps you physically engaged with an aggressor. Before you choose to restrain someone, consider carefully: do you have the legal necessity or authority to detain this person, and do you have the physical capability to maintain that restraint safely? In most civilian contexts, once the immediate threat has passed your legal obligation — and your best tactical option — is to disengage, put distance between yourself and the aggressor, and call law enforcement immediately. Restraint is appropriate when the threat is ongoing, physical disengagement is not possible, and you have the means to hold the person securely. If you are not trained, attempting a restraint hold on a resisting subject will more likely result in a ground scramble than a controlled detention.
</Note>

## The Wrist Lock (Kotegaeshi)

Kotegaeshi — "outward wrist turn" — is a wrist lock originating in Aikido and used widely in security and law enforcement for compliance and takedown. It works by rotating the wrist joint beyond its natural range of motion, creating pain compliance that can be used to guide an opponent to the ground.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Secure the Wrist">
    Get a firm two-handed grip on the opponent's wrist — one hand over the back of the hand, one around the wrist itself. Your grip needs to be secure before you apply any rotation: a loose grip allows them to pull free and turns the technique against you.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Rotate the Hand Outward">
    Rotate the hand so that the thumb points downward and the palm faces outward, away from the opponent's body. The rotation comes from your grip directing the hand — not from forcing the forearm. Move smoothly; the pain response begins quickly.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply Downward Pressure">
    Once the wrist is rotated, drive the hand downward toward the ground in an arc. This simultaneously loads the wrist joint and bends the opponent forward at the waist, disrupting their base.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Take the Opponent to the Ground">
    Continue the downward arc until the opponent is driven to the ground. Step toward them and out to the side as they fall to maintain leverage and avoid their free arm.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Maintain Wrist Control on the Ground">
    Keep the wrist rotated and controlled once they are down. Pin their hand to the mat palm-up and maintain pressure on the wrist joint. From here, you can issue verbal commands or transition to an escort hold.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
  Applied quickly or with excessive force, kotegaeshi will break the wrist. The technique should be applied with steady, deliberate pressure — not a snap. In training, your partner must be able to tap out and you must release instantly. In a real-world context, use only the force the situation requires.
</Warning>

## Arm Bar (Standing)

The standing arm bar — distinct from the ground submission of the same name — is an escort and compliance technique used routinely by police and security professionals to control a resisting subject and guide them to the ground or into handcuffs. It creates a mechanical lock on the elbow joint that the opponent cannot overcome without dislocating their own arm.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Control the Wrist and Elbow">
    Secure the opponent's wrist with one hand and place your other hand at the back of their elbow joint, just above the joint itself. Both control points are necessary — wrist without elbow gives them too much freedom; elbow without wrist provides no leverage.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Align the Joint Under Pressure">
    Drive the wrist upward while pressing the elbow downward, creating a hyperextension of the joint in the direction it cannot naturally flex. The opponent's arm should be relatively straight, with the elbow pointing toward the ground.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply Downward Pressure and Step Through">
    Step forward under the arm, driving your body weight downward through the elbow. This creates a pain response that forces the opponent to bend forward and lower their centre of gravity to relieve the pressure.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Escort to the Ground">
    Guide the opponent to the ground by continuing the downward drive and steering them by the wrist. As they reach the ground, maintain the lock to keep them compliant while you issue commands or transition to a wrist pin.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Rear Wrist Lock / Hammerlock

The hammerlock is performed by positioning the opponent's arm behind their back, with the wrist driven upward toward the shoulder blade. It is a standard law enforcement escort hold for compliant subjects: one officer controls the arm behind the back while escorting the subject forward.

To apply it: step behind the opponent, secure their wrist, and guide their arm behind their back so that the elbow bends and the hand rides up toward the opposite shoulder blade. Maintain control of the wrist throughout. With a cooperative subject, this is a manageable, low-force escort position.

<Warning>
  Forcing a non-compliant, actively resisting subject into a hammerlock — particularly by sharply jerking or yanking the arm up the back — creates a high risk of glenohumeral dislocation (shoulder dislocation) and rotator cuff tears. If the subject is fighting the hold, forcing more pressure is likely to injure them before it controls them. A non-compliant subject requires a different strategy: secure them first, then position the arm.
</Warning>

## The Rear Naked Choke (Blood Choke)

The rear naked choke (RNC) is a vascular restraint applied from back position. When applied correctly, it compresses the carotid arteries on both sides of the neck, reducing blood flow to the brain and producing unconsciousness within seconds. It is one of the most reliable finishing and restraint techniques in close-quarters scenarios — and one of the most misunderstood.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Achieve Back Position">
    Get behind the opponent with your chest against their back. You need back control — both in standing and ground scenarios — before the choke is viable. Do not attempt this from the front or side.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Position the Arm Across the Throat — Not the Windpipe">
    Slide your choking arm across the front of the neck so that the crook of your elbow is aligned with the centre of the throat. Your forearm should press against one carotid; the bicep against the other. The pressure must sit on the sides of the neck — not on the trachea at the front.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Place Your Hand Behind the Head">
    Bring your free hand behind the opponent's head, palm against the back of the skull. This hand will act as a lever to tighten the choke — it drives the head forward into the arm, increasing carotid compression without requiring you to squeeze harder.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Squeeze the Bicep and Forearm Together">
    Tighten the choke by squeezing your bicep and forearm inward while pressing the free hand forward against the back of the head. The pressure should be even on both sides. Do not crank the neck sideways or apply a twisting motion.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
  The rear naked choke produces unconsciousness in approximately four to ten seconds when applied correctly. Unconsciousness is not death — but holding the choke past the point of limpness risks hypoxic brain damage and, in prolonged cases, death. Release the choke the instant the subject goes limp. Immediately roll them into the recovery position, monitor their breathing, and call emergency services. Do not re-apply the choke once a person is unconscious. The RNC is a last-resort tool; its use must be proportionate to the threat.
</Warning>

## Communicating During Restraint

What you say during a restraint is as important as what you do physically. Panicking subjects often resist not because they intend to fight but because they do not understand what is happening or fear that you intend to harm them. Clear, calm verbal communication reduces the intensity of resistance and reduces the risk of injury to both parties.

Keep your commands short and direct: "Stop resisting." "I have you controlled — stop fighting." "Help is on the way." Avoid threats, insults, or anything that escalates their emotional state. A calm, authoritative tone signals that the situation is under control, which can itself induce compliance. Repeat commands as many times as needed without increasing aggression — your voice is a tool, not a weapon.

If bystanders are present, identify yourself clearly: "I am not attacking this person — they were the aggressor and I am holding them until police arrive." This protects you legally and prevents bystander intervention that could endanger everyone involved.

## After Restraint: Legal Considerations

<Note>
  The moment you have a person under physical restraint, call law enforcement immediately — do not wait. In most jurisdictions, the right of a private citizen to detain another person (citizen's arrest) is narrow, strictly defined, and carries significant legal liability if misapplied. Detaining someone for the wrong reasons, for longer than necessary, or in a manner that causes injury can expose you to civil and criminal liability even if the other person was the initial aggressor. Document as much as you can — note the time, the location, what the subject did, what force you used, and any witnesses present. Provide a clear, factual account to responding officers and cooperate fully. The restraint ends your physical obligation; the legal and procedural obligations that follow are equally important.
</Note>
