How you hold a knife and position your body determines what techniques are available to you and how much power you can generate. An incorrect grip limits your range of motion, telegraphs your movements, and increases the likelihood of losing the knife under the shock of contact. Stance determines your mobility, your ability to absorb a blow, and how quickly you can close or create distance. Grip and stance are the foundation of all knife skills — invest heavily in getting them right before moving to any technique.

## The Two Primary Grips

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Saber (Forward) Grip">
    The saber grip — also called the forward grip or hammer grip — is the most natural, versatile, and widely used grip in defensive knife systems. Hold the handle as you would a hammer: your thumb wraps around on the spine side of the blade, and the blade extends outward from the thumb side of your fist.

    **Characteristics:**
    - Blade extends toward your target from the thumb side of your hand
    - Natural, intuitive gripping motion — easily adopted under stress
    - Full range of cutting angles available: forehand, backhand, diagonal, and upward cuts
    - Strong thrusting capability — the wrist is aligned for direct forward extension
    - Longer effective reach than the reverse grip

    **When to use it:**
    - Recommended as the default defensive carry grip
    - Optimal for most defensive scenarios where you need versatility
    - Preferred grip in most Filipino Martial Arts systems for its range and adaptability

    To find the saber grip naturally under stress: close your hand as if catching a falling object. That natural fist orientation, with the blade extending away from your thumb, is the saber grip.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Icepick (Reverse) Grip">
    The icepick grip — also called the reverse grip or hammer-down grip — positions the blade extending downward from the pinky side of your fist. Hold the handle with all four fingers wrapped around it, blade pointing toward the ground and away from you when your arm hangs naturally.

    **Characteristics:**
    - Blade extends downward from the pinky side of the hand
    - Powerful downward striking and hooking cuts
    - Excellent for close-quarter grappling range where the arm is bent
    - Natural motion for a downward stabbing strike
    - Reduced effective reach compared to the saber grip
    - Limited thrusting capability — wrist alignment works against a direct forward thrust

    **When to use it:**
    - Effective in tight clinch range where full arm extension is not possible
    - Used in some Filipino Martial Arts systems for specific hooking techniques
    - Some practitioners prefer it for concealed deployment (blade faces the body when the hand is at the side)
    - Not generally recommended as a default defensive grip due to reduced versatility

    Practice transitioning between saber and reverse grips fluidly. Some advanced techniques flow from one to the other, and the ability to switch under pressure is a valuable skill — but only once each grip is independently solid.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Grip Pressure and Security

A knife in your hand is only useful if it stays there. Grip security under the shock of impact, during grappling, and across an extended physical confrontation is not guaranteed — you must train it deliberately.

Grip the handle firmly but not with maximum tension. A white-knuckle, maximum-effort grip causes rapid forearm fatigue, reduces the sensitivity you need to feel what your blade is doing, and telegraphs your intentions through visible muscle tension. A grip that is too loose risks losing the knife at the first point of hard contact with an attacker's arm or body.

The target grip pressure is "firmly present" — enough that the knife cannot be knocked free with a sudden blow, but not so tight that your forearm fatigues after 30 seconds of active movement. Think of how a carpenter holds a hammer: secure, controlled, and capable of sustained effort.

Your grip should also position the handle's pommel (butt end) against your palm, not your fingers. A handle that sits too far forward in the fingers provides less leverage and is easier to strip. Drive the handle back into your palm and close all four fingers around it.

## Fighting Stances

<Accordion title="High Guard">
  In the high guard, your knife hand is raised to approximately shoulder height, with the blade oriented toward your opponent. Your non-knife hand is raised in front of your face in a modified boxing guard position.

  **Purpose:** Protects the head and neck — the most immediately lethal target zones — from an incoming attack. Puts your weapon between you and the threat at close range.

  **Use it when:** The threat is close and presenting a weapon or raised hand; when you need to protect your face and head from an incoming strike or slash.

  **Limitation:** A raised knife hand is visible and identifiable to bystanders and may complicate legal justification. Transitions to strikes take a slightly longer path than from a middle guard.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Middle Guard">
  In the middle guard, your knife hand is held at chest height, roughly at your centreline, with the blade angled toward the opponent. Your non-knife hand is extended slightly forward as a reference guard.

  **Purpose:** Balanced position between offensive reach and defensive cover. The knife is in a neutral position from which you can deliver any cut, thrust, or defensive parry without a large preparatory movement.

  **Use it when:** You are unsure what attack is coming and need to be ready for anything. The middle guard is the most commonly taught starting position in Filipino Martial Arts and is a reliable default.

  **Limitation:** Less protective of the head than a high guard; requires deliberate movement to cover a high incoming attack.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Low Guard">
  In the low guard, your knife hand is held at or below waist height, with the blade partially concealed behind your thigh or pointed toward the ground. Your non-knife hand may be raised in a more open, non-threatening gesture.

  **Purpose:** Conceals the weapon from your opponent at conversational distance. This guard is used in the period before a weapon is needed — when you are aware of a potential threat and have the knife in hand, but the situation has not yet forced a confrontation.

  **Use it when:** You believe a threat may develop but has not yet become immediate; when you want to maintain concealment of your weapon while being ready to deploy it instantly.

  **Limitation:** Slower to bring into an active defensive posture than either the middle or high guard. Not appropriate once active combat has begun.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Defanging Stance">
  The defanging stance positions your body with your non-dominant side angled forward toward the opponent, reducing your overall target profile. Your knife hand is behind your lead shoulder, somewhat concealed by your body angle.

  **Purpose:** Minimises the target your body presents while keeping your weapon available. Reduces the likelihood of taking a cut to a vital area by presenting a narrower profile.

  **Use it when:** Moving offline from an attack; as a transitional stance when circling or repositioning; in combination with triangle footwork to create angles.

  **Limitation:** Less intuitive for most people; requires specific training to use fluidly. Do not attempt to fight exclusively from this stance without substantial practice.
</Accordion>

## Footwork Principles

Footwork in knife fighting follows the same fundamental rule as all combative movement: never cross your feet. Crossed feet destroy your balance, leave you momentarily unable to generate power, and make it easy for an attacker to take you off your feet.

The primary footwork pattern in Filipino Martial Arts is **triangle stepping**. Instead of moving straight forward or backward, you step at angles — forward-offline, backward-offline, and laterally — following an imaginary triangle on the floor. This keeps you moving off the line of attack rather than directly into or away from it.

Key principles:
- **Advance:** Step your lead foot forward first, then bring your rear foot up to restore your base — never drag your feet together
- **Retreat:** Step your rear foot back first, then bring your lead foot back to restore your base
- **Offline angles:** Step at 45° angles rather than straight lines; this takes you out of the attacker's direct line while maintaining your proximity to counter
- **Maintain shoulder-width base:** Do not let your feet come closer than shoulder-width at any point during a movement; this is your stability foundation
- **Weight distribution:** Stay on the balls of your feet, not your heels; heels-down stance makes you slow to move and easy to push off balance

## Training Drill: Mirror Work

One of the most effective solo practice methods for grips and stances is mirror work. Stand before a full-length mirror and practice the following sequence:

Begin in a neutral standing position with your training knife at your side. Assume the saber grip and move into the middle guard. Hold the position and check your grip, your elbow position, your shoulder height, and your body angle. Then transition deliberately to the high guard, pause and check, then to the low guard, then back to middle. Repeat the circuit, adding the defanging stance.

Next, add footwork: triangle step forward-left while moving from middle to high guard, then retreat backward-right to middle, then step offline-right to low guard. Keep the movements smooth and controlled — speed comes later. The goal of mirror work is to see what your posture actually looks like versus what you think it looks like. Most practitioners are surprised by the gap. Practise for 10 minutes per session until every transition is clean, balanced, and natural.
