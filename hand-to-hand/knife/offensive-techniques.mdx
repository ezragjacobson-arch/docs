Offensive knife techniques are delivered in response to an imminent physical threat when escape is not possible. The goal is not to inflict maximum damage or to dominate a fight — it is to stop the threat quickly enough to escape to safety. The cuts, thrusts, and targeting frameworks on this page are drawn from Filipino Martial Arts (Kali/Escrima), Krav Maga, and military combatives, and are presented solely for lawful self-defence education.

<Warning>
  The techniques described on this page are presented strictly for defensive purposes. Applying them against another person outside of a lawful act of self-defence constitutes a serious criminal offence — potentially aggravated assault, grievous bodily harm, or worse depending on the outcome and your jurisdiction. Study these techniques to understand and defend against them, and to be prepared to act lawfully when your life is in immediate danger. Nothing on this page authorises, endorses, or encourages violence.
</Warning>

Every technique on this page is a tool for one singular purpose: stopping the threat as quickly as possible so you can escape to safety. Understand the intent before you study the mechanics.

## Targeting Principles

Effective defensive knife use targets the body's mechanical systems — the motor nerves, tendons, blood vessels, and structural supports that allow an attacker to continue harming you. Targeting these systems ends the threat efficiently and without requiring a precision strike under extreme stress.

The primary defensive targeting hierarchy, from first choice to last:

1. **Weapon limb and weapon hand:** Cutting the attacker's weapon arm, hand, or wrist may cause them to drop their weapon or lose the ability to control it. This is the defanging principle and it is the preferred first response. You are attacking the threat, not the person.
2. **Legs and lower extremities:** Cuts to the femoral artery (inner thigh) or tendons behind the knee can stop forward movement rapidly. Targets on the legs create significant physiological effect without requiring you to close inside striking range.
3. **Arms and shoulders:** Cuts to the bicep, forearm tendons, and the medial (inner arm) blood supply degrade the attacker's physical capability without requiring precision that is impossible under adrenal stress.
4. **Torso and neck:** Vital targets that can produce rapid incapacitation but carry the highest legal and moral weight. Resort to these only when no other target is accessible and the threat to your life is immediate and unavoidable.

Your training should internalise this hierarchy so that targeting decisions happen automatically during a confrontation. You do not have time to reason through it in the moment.

## The 12 Angles of Attack (Kali/Escrima)

Filipino Martial Arts systems — particularly Kali (also called Escrima or Arnis) — organise all possible weapon attacks into a numbered system of 12 angles. This framework is not an exhaustive catalogue of techniques; it is a training map that ensures you have practised covering and delivering attacks from every direction. Once you understand the angles, you understand every possible incoming attack and have a response for each one.

The 12 angles are grouped into families based on direction of travel:

| Angle(s) | Direction | Delivery |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Diagonal downward | 1: Forehand high-to-low; 2: Backhand high-to-low |
| 3–4 | Horizontal | 3: Forehand across body; 4: Backhand across body |
| 5–6 | Diagonal upward | 5: Forehand low-to-high; 6: Backhand low-to-high |
| 7–8 | Thrusts | 7: Low thrust; 8: High thrust |
| 9–12 | Additional angles | Downward vertical, upward vertical, and variations |

When you drill the 12 angles with a training knife — both delivering and receiving/parrying each one — you build a comprehensive library of motion that your body can access under stress. The specific numbering varies slightly between Kali lineages; what matters is the completeness of coverage, not the numbers themselves.

## Fundamental Cuts

<Accordion title="Forehand Diagonal Cut">
  The forehand diagonal cut travels from the high outside (your weapon-hand shoulder side) down toward the low inside (your non-weapon-hand hip side). It follows the same motion as a natural hammering or chopping stroke, making it one of the most powerful and instinctive cuts available.

  **Execution:** From the saber grip, raise your weapon hand to approximately shoulder height on your weapon side. Drive the blade down and across your body in a diagonal arc, finishing at approximately hip height on the opposite side. Keep your elbow slightly bent at the point of contact to prevent hyperextension.

  **Targets:** Forearm, bicep, collarbone area, neck (high diagonal), across the chest or abdomen (mid diagonal).

  **Training note:** This is Angle 1 in most Kali numbering systems. It is typically the first cut taught and the foundation from which all other cuts are developed.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Backhand Diagonal Cut">
  The backhand diagonal cut mirrors the forehand diagonal, traveling from the high inside (your non-weapon-hand side) downward to the low outside. It is a natural follow-up to the forehand cut and flows directly from the finishing position of that strike.

  **Execution:** From the finishing position of the forehand diagonal, immediately reverse the motion — drive the blade back diagonally upward across your body, then let it fall naturally into a backhand diagonal cut from high inside to low outside. Practise the forehand-backhand combination until the transition is seamless.

  **Targets:** The reverse of the forehand diagonal — weapon arm (from the inside), opposite side of the neck, cross-body torso.

  **Training note:** This is Angle 2 in the standard Kali numbering. The forehand-backhand combination (Angles 1 and 2) is the most fundamental two-count drill in Filipino Martial Arts.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Horizontal Slash">
  The horizontal slash travels in a level plane across the body — either forehand (weapon-side to non-weapon-side) or backhand. It is delivered at whatever height the target presents, most commonly at arm/forearm height, waist height, or neck height.

  **Execution:** From your guard, drive the blade in a flat, level arc across your body. Keep your elbow generating the power from behind the wrist — avoid a limp-wristed delivery that telegraphs the cut and reduces cutting power.

  **Targets:** Forearm and wrist (weapon-arm targeting), abdomen (waist height), throat and neck (high horizontal).

  **Training note:** Angles 3 and 4 in the Kali system. Highly effective as an interception cut against a straight punch or jabbing attack.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Upward Cut">
  The upward cut travels from low to high — a rising arc that can deliver significant power and is extremely effective at targeting the weapon limb, as an attacker's arm extending toward you presents an ideal upward-cut target.

  **Execution:** From a low guard or from the finishing position of a previous cut, drive the blade upward in an arc, rotating your forearm so the blade faces forward at the point of contact. The motion resembles an uppercut punch.

  **Targets:** Inner forearm and wrist (weapon-arm targeting), inside of the elbow joint, underarm area.

  **Training note:** Angles 5 and 6 in the Kali system. The upward cut is particularly useful as an interception against a downward strike, cutting the attacking arm as it descends toward you.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Thrust">
  The thrust is a direct, forward stab delivered along the centreline of your body. It is the most anatomically damaging delivery in knife combat and produces wound channels that no slashing cut can replicate. It is also the most committed — a missed or partially committed thrust puts you off-balance and vulnerable.

  **Execution:** From the saber grip, extend your weapon arm directly forward in a straight line, driving the point toward your target. Step through with your weapon-side foot to add body weight behind the thrust. Commit fully — a half-hearted thrust is worse than no thrust.

  **Targets:** Solar plexus, throat, and other vital targets. The thrust is a last-resort technique against vital targets when stopping the threat is immediately necessary.

  **Critical note:** Commit fully or do not thrust at all. A half-committed thrust that does not reach the target still creates a gaping opening in your own defence. Train the commitment as a separate discipline — the decision to thrust must be made completely before the motion begins.
</Accordion>

## The Defanging Principle

Defanging — a term drawn directly from Kali/Escrima tradition — means targeting the opponent's weapon hand, wrist, and weapon arm before attacking their body. The logic is sound and practical: an attacker who cannot hold their weapon cannot use it against you. Cutting the weapon hand or forearm may cause involuntary drop of the weapon, even against a determined attacker, due to severed tendons or nerve damage.

Practise targeting the weapon limb first in every training drill. When your training partner extends a punch, your first response should be to cut the arm. When they swing a training weapon, your first response is to cut the attacking hand on the way in. Make this instinctive. The mindset shift from "I need to stab my attacker" to "I need to destroy their ability to hurt me" is one of the most important conceptual changes in defensive knife training.

Defanging also carries a significant legal and moral advantage: targeting a limb rather than vital anatomy demonstrates proportionality and defensive intent, which matters enormously in any subsequent legal proceeding.

## Combination Work

Single techniques rarely decide a confrontation. Effective defensive knife use involves combinations — linked sequences of responses that flow naturally from one movement to the next. The following is a foundational defensive combination to internalise first.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Parry or Deflect the Incoming Attack">
    Your opponent initiates. Do not freeze and do not meet force with force. Redirect the incoming attack — use your non-weapon hand or weapon arm to deflect it offline, away from your centreline. Step offline simultaneously using triangle footwork.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Deliver Forehand Cut to the Weapon Limb">
    Immediately after the deflection, deliver a forehand diagonal cut to the attacker's weapon arm or hand. This is your defanging response. Do not wait to see if the deflection was successful before beginning your counter — train the counter to be simultaneous with the parry.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Follow with Backhand Cut or Thrust">
    Flow from the forehand cut directly into a backhand cut — the natural continuation of the forehand's arc — or, if the attacker has closed distance and is still presenting a threat, commit to a thrust. Do not pause between cuts.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Create Distance and Assess">
    After delivering your combination, push off the attacker and create distance. Move offline, not straight backward. From a few feet away, reassess: is the threat neutralised? Is the attacker still mobile and dangerous? Are there additional threats?
  </Step>
  <Step title="Escape if Possible">
    If an exit is available, take it immediately. The goal was never to "win" — it was to stop the threat and survive. Once you have created distance and the attacker is no longer pursuing, run.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tip>
  Practise every technique on this page using a rubber or aluminium training knife, under the supervision of a qualified instructor. The physical sensations of a real confrontation — adrenaline, tunnel vision, fine motor degradation — cannot be replicated in solo drilling, but they can be approximated through pressure testing with a resisting partner. Book time with a Kali, Escrima, or Filipino Martial Arts instructor who includes alive training (padded sparring with resistance) in their curriculum.
</Tip>
