Knife defence is one of the most demanding skill sets in close-quarters self-defence. This page covers the responses drawn from Filipino Martial Arts (Kali, Escrima), Krav Maga, and military combatives — the three systems with the most documented, tested methodology for edged weapon defence. These are the techniques that give you a structured, trained response rather than panic-driven improvisation when facing an armed attacker.

<Warning>
  Defending against an armed attacker is extremely dangerous. You will likely sustain injury even if your defence is technically correct. This is not a training caveat — it is a physiological reality. The techniques on this page can save your life, but escape is always the preferred option. Only engage physically if you are cornered, cannot run, or are defending someone who cannot run. If there is any avenue of escape, take it.
</Warning>

If you cannot escape a knife-armed attacker, you need a strategy. Freezing is not a strategy — it is a guarantee of a worse outcome. This page covers the defensive responses drawn from Filipino Martial Arts (Kali, Escrima), Krav Maga, and military combatives: the three systems with the most documented, tested methodology for edged weapon defence. These techniques will not make you invulnerable. They will give you a structured, trained response that is significantly better than improvisation under terror.

## The First Principle: Escape

Running is the best knife defence. Say it out loud and mean it. No technique in this document is as effective as putting a car, a wall, a crowd, or a locked door between you and an attacker with a knife.

If escape is available, pursue it without hesitation. Drop your bag, kick off your shoes if necessary, and run. The ego cost of running from a confrontation is zero compared to the cost of being cut. Many people who freeze and fight when they could have escaped do so because of pride or disbelief — "this can't really be happening." Accept that it is happening, make the decision immediately, and run.

Only when escape is genuinely not available — you are in a room with one exit that the attacker controls, you are protecting a child or an incapacitated person, you are already in contact — does active defensive technique become necessary.

## Against an Unarmed Defender vs. Armed Attacker

<Note>
  If you do not have a defensive tool of your own, do not attempt to defend against a knife with bare hands unless it is your only option. Before going empty-handed against a blade, look for anything in your environment you can use as a barrier, shield, or equaliser. A backpack held by the straps can absorb a slash. A jacket wrapped around your forearm provides modest protection. A chair held with all four legs facing outward keeps a knife at distance far more effectively than your bare arms. A heavy bag, a briefcase, a bicycle — any object that keeps distance between the blade and your body is a defensive tool.

  Using an improvised shield does not mean "winning" — it means surviving long enough to escape or for help to arrive.
</Note>

## Parrying and Deflecting

Do not attempt to "block" a knife. A block — a rigid, meeting-force-with-force stop of the attacking limb — is appropriate against an empty-hand strike where the worst outcome is pain. Against a blade, a block that fails to completely stop the arm means the knife reaches you anyway, and a block that succeeds still puts the blade in dangerous proximity to your stopping hand.

The correct principle is **redirection**: you want to move the attacking limb offline — away from your body's centreline — rather than stopping it in place. Redirecting requires less strength, works even when your deflection is not perfectly timed, and opens the attacker's structure for your counter-response.

Redirect the weapon arm at the wrist or forearm with a slapping, pushing, or hooking motion. Aim to move the blade away from your body while simultaneously stepping offline. Your deflection and your footwork must work together — deflecting without moving your body only creates a momentary margin, and margins are narrow against a blade.

## Key Defensive Responses

<Accordion title='Forehand Parry ("Roof Block")'>
  The forehand parry — sometimes called the roof block — defends against a downward stabbing attack (Angle 1 in the Kali numbering system, or a classic "ice-pick overhead stab").

  **Execution:**
  1. As the attack descends, raise your knife-hand forearm to intercept the attacker's wrist or forearm from the outside.
  2. Deflect the attacking arm outward and to your weapon-side — away from your centreline.
  3. Simultaneously step offline to your weapon side, taking yourself off the line of the descending blade.
  4. Immediately deliver a counter-cut to the attacker's weapon arm or a hammer strike to their elbow joint.

  **Key point:** Your parrying surface is the hard bone of your outer forearm, not your palm or fingers. Keep your fingers together and your wrist firm. Contact with the attacker's forearm or wrist — not their hand — gives you the most control.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Backhand Parry">
  The backhand parry defends against a horizontal inward slash (Angle 4 — backhand horizontal, attacking your centreline from the outside).

  **Execution:**
  1. As the slash comes inward, use your non-weapon hand (or weapon arm in a backhand position) to deflect the attacking forearm outward — away from your body.
  2. Step offline simultaneously, moving away from the direction the blade is travelling.
  3. The deflection redirects the slash past your body. Follow immediately with a counter-cut to the weapon limb or a push to create distance.

  **Key point:** Your step offline is not optional. A backhand parry without a simultaneous step merely deflects the blade past you — any error in the deflection and the blade still contacts you. The step creates the actual margin.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Inside-Outside Parry">
  The inside-outside parry is a two-count response from Kali that covers both the inside and outside lines in a single, fluid sequence. It is used against a committed forward thrust (Angles 7–8) or a rapid combination attack.

  **Execution (two-count):**
  1. **Count 1 (inside parry):** Deflect the incoming thrust to the outside with your lead hand, redirecting the weapon arm away from your centreline.
  2. **Count 2 (outside parry):** Without pausing, use your second hand to hook and control the attacker's weapon arm from the outside, trapping it and preventing withdrawal for re-attack.
  3. With the weapon arm trapped, deliver your counter immediately — a cut to the arm, a knee strike, or enter the disarm sequence.

  **Key point:** The two-count must flow as a single continuous motion, not two separate pauses. Drill it slowly until it is one smooth action, then build speed.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Clinch and Control">
  At very close range — inside arm's reach — slashing cuts lose most of their effectiveness because the attacker cannot build arc distance. Closing distance aggressively (rather than backing away) can temporarily neutralise the blade.

  **Execution:**
  1. Enter aggressively off the line of attack — step inside and past the blade, not into it.
  2. Wrap the attacker's weapon arm with your non-dominant arm, trapping it against your body or controlling it at the elbow.
  3. Use your other hand to deliver short-range strikes (palm heel, elbow, headbutt) while maintaining control of the weapon arm.
  4. Transition to a disarm or takedown if conditions allow; if not, push off and create distance to reassess.

  **Key point:** Clinch-and-control buys time and reduces immediate danger but does not end the threat. It is a transitional tool, not a finishing solution. Transition to a disarm or escape as quickly as possible.
</Accordion>

## Disarming Techniques

Disarming a knife-armed attacker is one of the most difficult physical skills in close-quarters defence. On a compliant training partner, disarms look clean and reliable. Against a determined, resisting attacker under full adrenal load, they are difficult, unreliable, and dangerous. Pursue a disarm only when you have sufficient control of the weapon arm to make it viable — not as a first move from a distance.

The following sequence describes a fundamental disarm against a forward thrust (straight stab):

<Steps>
  <Step title="Step Offline at 45°">
    As the thrust comes forward, step at a 45° angle to the outside of the weapon arm — do not step straight backward. Stepping offline takes you off the line of the blade's travel and positions you advantageously relative to the attacker's body structure.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Parry and Redirect the Weapon Arm Outward">
    Simultaneously with your step, use your lead hand to deflect the weapon arm outward — away from your body centreline. Aim for the wrist or forearm. Your goal is to redirect, not to stop. The blade should pass to the outside of your body.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Grab the Weapon Wrist with Both Hands">
    Immediately after the deflection, secure the weapon wrist with both hands. Grip firmly at the wrist — not at the hand or fingers, which are too small and mobile. Your grip should be secure enough that the attacker cannot easily pull the arm back or rotate the blade toward you.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply a Wrist Lever or Arm Bar">
    With control of the wrist, apply mechanical leverage to force release of the knife:
    - **Wrist flexion:** Fold the wrist sharply downward and inward — the pain of wrist flexion against a locked wrist joint typically causes release.
    - **Arm bar:** Extend the attacker's arm fully, roll the elbow joint upward against its natural direction, and apply downward pressure. The knife hand will naturally open.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Strip the Knife and Create Distance">
    As the knife loosens, sweep it away from the attacker's hand with a downward or lateral motion — strip it, do not catch it. Immediately create distance: push the attacker away and move offline. Do not stand still with their weapon in your hand — move, reassess, and escape if possible.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
  Disarms performed on compliant training partners look very different from disarms performed against a resisting attacker. In a real scenario, focus your energy on controlling the weapon arm and preventing it from reaching your vital areas — not on achieving the clean disarm technique you practised in the gym. A messy, partial disarm that keeps the blade away from your body is more valuable than a failed perfect technique. Train the mechanics, but do not let "I need to achieve the disarm" become a fixation that distracts you from the primary objective: survival.
</Warning>

## Improvised Shields

When you do not have a defensive tool and disarming is not yet viable, improvised shields and barriers are your first line of defence. They keep the blade at a distance and absorb slashes and stabs that would otherwise reach your body.

Common improvised shields and how to use them:

- **Jacket or coat:** Remove quickly and wrap around your non-dominant forearm as padding, or hold it outstretched between you and the attacker to create a visual barrier and absorb incoming cuts
- **Backpack or bag:** Hold by the straps and present the body of the bag facing outward; a rigid-framed pack can stop or significantly slow a slash; thrust attacks may penetrate but still slow the blade
- **Briefcase or hard-sided case:** Hold with both hands by the handle and present the flat face toward the attacker; use it actively to strike and push rather than just holding it passively
- **Chair:** Hold by the seat with all four legs pointing outward toward the attacker; four hard points create distance and prevent easy approach; this is the standard animal-trainer technique for a reason
- **Any rigid object:** A laptop, a book, a piece of furniture — anything you can hold between yourself and the blade extends your effective defence perimeter

The goal of an improvised shield is not to win — it is to absorb, delay, and create the moment for escape. Use the shield to keep the attacker at distance, absorb their first committed attack, and then run.

<Tip>
  The most effective preparation for knife defence is a dedicated course taught by a qualified instructor in a system specifically designed for edged weapon scenarios. Krav Maga (both IKMF and KMG lineages), Filipino Martial Arts with a focus on defensive application (Inosanto Kali, Pekiti-Tirsia, Sayoc Kali), and Dog Brothers Martial Arts all teach edged weapon defence with regular alive training against resisting partners. A weekend seminar with a reputable instructor will give you more functional skill than months of solo drilling. Find a course, train regularly, and pressure-test what you learn.
</Tip>
