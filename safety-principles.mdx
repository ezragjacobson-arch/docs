Safety is not a section you read once and move past — it is the foundation every technique in this guide is built on. Whether you are handling a firearm, practising a takedown, or running a de-escalation scenario, the principles on this page apply without exception. Internalise them before you progress to any other content, and return to them regularly as your training deepens.

---

## The Four Firearm Safety Rules

These four rules are universal across every shooting discipline, training environment, and jurisdiction. Treat them as absolute — there are no exceptions.

**1. Treat every firearm as if it is loaded.**

<Note>
  Never assume a firearm is unloaded based on memory, someone else's word, or a previous check. Every time you pick up a firearm — even one you set down thirty seconds ago — treat it as live. This rule eliminates the most common cause of negligent discharges.
</Note>

**2. Never point a firearm at anything you are not prepared to destroy.**

<Note>
  Muzzle discipline means the muzzle never sweeps a person, animal, or object you are unwilling to put a round through. Maintain conscious awareness of where your muzzle is pointing at all times, even during administrative handling, holstering, and unloading.
</Note>

**3. Keep your finger off the trigger until your sights are on target.**

<Note>
  Your trigger finger rests straight along the frame, outside the trigger guard, until you have made a conscious decision to fire. This is the single most important mechanical habit you can build. It prevents negligent discharges caused by startle responses, stumbles, and grip adjustments.
</Note>

**4. Know your target and what lies beyond it.**

<Note>
  A projectile that misses or passes through a target continues travelling. Before you fire, identify your target positively and assess what is in the background and beyond. In a defensive scenario this awareness can be the difference between a lawful act of self-defence and a tragedy.
</Note>

## Training Safety

Physical training carries its own risk profile. Follow these rules every time you train, regardless of your experience level.

- **Always train with a qualified instructor.** Beginner or advanced, working with a certified coach ensures technique is correct from the start and dangerous habits are caught early.
- **Use proper protective equipment.** Eye and ear protection for firearms training, appropriate mats and padding for grappling and takedowns, and appropriate gloves or guards for contact drills.
- **Never practise techniques at full force without supervision.** High-intensity drilling without an experienced eye present increases the risk of serious injury to you and your training partner.
- **Tap out and speak up.** If you are in pain, feeling dizzy, or emotionally distressed during training, stop immediately and communicate it. There is no value in pushing through an injury or a mental health moment.
- **Stop immediately if someone is hurt.** Cease all activity, assess the injury, and provide or call for appropriate medical assistance before resuming any training.

## Mental and Emotional Readiness

Physical technique is only part of self-defence competence. The psychological dimension is equally important — and far less discussed.

Effective self-defence requires you to function under acute stress. Adrenaline narrows your field of vision, degrades fine motor skills, accelerates your heart rate, and distorts your perception of time. Stress inoculation training — gradually exposing yourself to higher-pressure training scenarios under professional supervision — helps your nervous system build tolerance so you can think more clearly when it matters most.

Managing the aftermath of a confrontation is equally important. Even lawful, proportionate use of force can produce significant psychological trauma. Build a support network in advance: know who your doctor is, understand your organisation's or state's critical incident support resources, and do not dismiss the emotional weight of a self-defence event.

Above all, develop a clear decision-making framework for when **not** to act. Ego, pride, and the desire to "win" an argument are not self-defence justifications. A genuine self-defence situation involves an imminent, credible threat to your safety or the safety of another person. If that threshold is not met, disengage.

## Emergency Response

If you are involved in a self-defence incident, your actions in the immediate aftermath are critical — legally and medically. Follow these steps in order.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Ensure the Scene Is Safe">
    Scan your surroundings for additional threats before rendering aid or making calls. A second threat you missed is more dangerous than the one you just addressed. Move to a position of safety if the environment is still dynamic.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Call Emergency Services">
    Call your national emergency number immediately. Give your location, describe what happened factually and briefly, report any injuries, and follow the dispatcher's instructions. Being the first caller establishes your role as the person who sought help.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Seek Medical Attention">
    Check yourself and anyone around you for injuries. Adrenaline masks pain — wounds you did not notice in the moment may be serious. Accept medical assessment from responding paramedics even if you feel uninjured.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Do Not Make Statements Without Legal Counsel">
    You have the right to remain silent. Provide your basic identification to law enforcement as required by your jurisdiction, but do not give a detailed account of the incident until you have spoken with a lawyer. Statements made under acute stress are often imprecise and can be used against you.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tip>
  Take a certified first aid course — ideally one that includes trauma response, tourniquet application, and CPR. In an emergency, professional help may be minutes away. The skills to stabilise an injury in the interim can save a life, including your own.
</Tip>
