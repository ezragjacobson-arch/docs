Running is not cowardice — it is often the smartest, safest, and legally cleanest decision you can make in a threatening situation. Every moment you spend in proximity to a threat increases your risk. This page covers how to create distance, disengage from physical contact, and remove yourself from danger with purpose and speed.

## The Escape Mindset

The most common barrier to effective escape is not physical — it is psychological. The stress response that floods your system when danger appears can produce a powerful freeze reaction: your body locks up, your mind goes blank, and you remain rooted to the spot while precious seconds pass.

You override the freeze response through mental preparation. When you have already decided — in advance, in safety — that your first response to a threat will always be to look for an exit, your brain has a ready-made action to execute when adrenaline hits. Preparation bypasses the need for in-the-moment decision-making. The time to choose your response is now, not when you are face to face with a threat.

Practise asking yourself regularly in everyday environments: *"If something happened right now, where would I go?"* This builds the reflex of always knowing your exits before you need them.

<Note>
  Many law enforcement and personal safety organisations teach a simple three-tier priority framework: **Run, Hide, Fight** — in that order. If you can run, run. If you cannot run, hide and conceal yourself. Fighting is the last option when the first two are not available. This hierarchy keeps escape as your default and reserve physical defence for when no other option exists.
</Note>

## Creating Distance

Physical distance is your most valuable asset in any threatening situation. Every metre of space between you and a threat is time — time to assess, to call for help, and to prepare your next move.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Use a verbal command to buy time">
    A loud, clear command — *"Stay back!"* or *"Stop right there!"* — serves multiple purposes simultaneously. It may startle or pause the aggressor long enough for you to begin moving. It signals to bystanders that something is wrong and that you need help. And it establishes, for any later legal or witness account, that you attempted to avoid the confrontation. Project from your diaphragm. Volume and authority matter.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Put a physical barrier between you and the threat">
    Use whatever your environment offers — a parked car, a table, a bollard, a crowd of people. Even a second of obstruction can change your survival odds. Move toward the barrier immediately and keep it between you as you continue toward your exit. Do not treat the barrier as cover; treat it as a tool to buy movement.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Move at an angle, not straight backwards">
    Moving directly backward is slow, unstable, and easy to pursue. Step off-line at a 45-degree angle — this disrupts the aggressor's targeting, creates lateral distance more quickly than retreating, and positions you to turn and run once you have created initial separation.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Move toward people and light">
    Aggressors are deterred by witnesses and visibility. Move toward crowds, staffed premises, lit areas, and road traffic. A busy street, an open shop, or a lit car park all change the risk calculus for someone who wants to harm you without consequence.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Do not turn your back until you have significant distance">
    Turning and sprinting too early leaves you vulnerable to being grabbed, tripped, or struck from behind. Maintain visual contact and continue moving laterally and off-line until you have created enough distance that the threat cannot close it before you can reach safety. Then run, and run hard.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Breaking Common Grabs

If a threat closes to contact distance before you can escape, you may need to break a grab to create the distance required to flee. These techniques are designed for one purpose: to free yourself and run. They are not fighting techniques — they are escape tools.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Wrist Grab">
    When an aggressor grabs your wrist, their grip is weakest at the point where the thumb and forefinger meet. Use this structural weakness to escape.

    **Step 1:** Do not pull backward — this is the direction they expect and can easily hold against. Instead, rotate your arm so your elbow moves upward and your wrist turns toward the inside of their thumb.

    **Step 2:** As you rotate, drive your arm sharply and directly through the gap between their thumb and fingers in one explosive motion. The force required is significantly less than trying to pull free against the full grip.

    **Step 3:** Use the momentum of your arm pull to turn your body away and step into your escape route. Do not pause — convert the break directly into movement.

    **Practise note:** Drill this movement slowly with a partner before adding speed. The rotation direction must become automatic so it executes correctly under stress.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Bear Hug (Arms Free)">
    A bear hug with your arms free — seized from behind with the attacker's arms around your torso but above your own arms — leaves you with significant physical tools to work with.

    **Step 1:** Drop your weight immediately by bending your knees and widening your stance. This lowers your centre of gravity, making you harder to lift or control, and brings your feet into weapon range.

    **Step 2:** Stamp sharply downward onto the instep of the attacker's foot with the heel of your shoe. Aim for the top of the foot. This produces significant pain and often loosens the grip.

    **Step 3:** If the grip remains, drive the back of your head sharply into the attacker's face. Keep your chin tucked and drive with the solid rear portion of your skull, not the top of your head.

    **Step 4:** As their grip loosens, step sharply to the side — not forward — to break out of their arms. Turn to face them briefly to create your angle of escape, then move immediately toward your exit.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Collar / Shirt Grab">
    A collar or shirt grab — where the attacker seizes the front of your clothing with one or both hands — is a controlling technique designed to hold you in place or set up a strike.

    **Step 1:** React immediately — delay allows them to establish a stronger grip or follow through with a strike. Speed is more important than perfection.

    **Step 2:** Bring both your hands up and inside their grabbing arms. Place both palms firmly against the back or sides of their grabbing hands to pin them momentarily against your clothing.

    **Step 3:** Drive both arms sharply outward and downward in a single explosive movement — think of the motion as "opening a book" but forcefully downward. This breaks the grip by forcing the fingers open against their natural range of movement.

    **Step 4:** Simultaneously step backward or off-line to create space. Do not wait to see their reaction — move the moment the grip breaks and do not stop until you reach safety.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Vehicle and Transit Safety

Many threatening situations occur in or around vehicles and public transport — environments that limit your options and often isolate you from help. Apply heightened awareness and specific habits in these contexts.

In car parks, approach your vehicle with your keys already in your hand and your attention on the surrounding area — not your phone. Scan the spaces around your vehicle as you approach and check the rear passenger compartment before entering. If someone is loitering near your car without obvious purpose, do not approach. Return to the building, call ahead for an escort, or wait for the person to leave.

On public transport, sit near the driver or conductor when possible, particularly on night services. Keep bags on your lap rather than on the floor or overhead rack. If you feel followed when you leave, do not go home — go to a busy public space and call for help. A person who follows you off two or more stops is following you deliberately.

In rideshares, always verify the vehicle plate, make, model, and the driver's profile photo before getting in. Share your trip details — including the route — with someone you trust. Sit in the back seat rather than the front. If the driver takes an unexpected route, question it calmly but immediately, and be prepared to ask to be let out in a populated area.

## Safe Haven Locations

When running, have a destination in mind. Certain locations offer safety not just through crowds but through staffing, access to emergency services, and a duty-of-care obligation that means staff will assist you.

- **Police stations** — the most direct refuge; an aggressor will rarely follow you inside
- **Fire stations** — staffed around the clock and equipped to assist in emergencies
- **Hospital emergency departments** — always staffed, always open, always obligated to help
- **Busy restaurants, cafés, and bars** — staff have a legal duty to assist and there are witnesses
- **Any staffed public building** — libraries, council offices, shopping centre security points
- **Pharmacies and supermarkets** — typically staffed, well-lit, and with CCTV coverage

In all cases, enter, move toward staff immediately, and clearly state that you need help. Do not assume they will understand the situation without being told directly.

<Warning>
  The grab-escape techniques described on this page are simplified introductions to physical defence concepts. They require repeated physical practice with a qualified, experienced instructor to be reliable under the extreme stress and adrenaline of a real attack. Reading about a technique gives you awareness; training it gives you capability. Seek out a reputable self-defence school, reality-based martial arts programme, or law enforcement-style personal safety course to build these skills properly.
</Warning>
