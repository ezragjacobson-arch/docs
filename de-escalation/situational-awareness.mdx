The best self-defence is never having to use it. Situational awareness — the practiced ability to read your environment, notice what is out of place, and identify potential threats early — gives you the one resource that no physical technique can replace: time. When you see danger developing before it peaks, you can avoid it, reposition, or act decisively rather than react in panic.

## Cooper's Color Code

Developed by Lieutenant Colonel Jeff Cooper, the Color Code is a simple mental framework for describing your state of awareness at any given moment. It is not about paranoia — it is about calibrating your attention appropriately to your environment.

**White — Unaware:** You are relaxed and oblivious to your surroundings. Absorbed in your phone, asleep, zoned out. This is appropriate at home in a secure environment. In public, White is dangerous — you will not see a threat coming until it is already upon you.

**Yellow — Relaxed Alertness:** You are calm but observant. You are taking in your surroundings without fixating on any single threat. This is the baseline state you should maintain whenever you are in public. You are not anxious or hypervigilant — you simply know what is around you.

**Orange — Specific Alert:** Something has drawn your attention. A person is behaving unusually. A situation feels wrong. You have a specific, identified cause for concern and are mentally preparing your response options. You are watching the situation develop.

**Red — Ready to Act:** A threat has been confirmed. You are either executing a pre-planned response — escape, verbal command, physical defence — or you are actively engaged. Decision-making time is over; action time has begun.

<Note>
  Most people live almost entirely in White when out in public. Your goal is to make Yellow your default whenever you leave a secured environment. Yellow does not mean fearful or suspicious of everyone — it simply means present, engaged, and informed about what is around you.
</Note>

## Pre-Attack Indicators

Violent attacks rarely occur without warning. Experienced self-defence instructors, law enforcement officers, and security professionals have identified a consistent cluster of behaviours that frequently precede a physical assault. Train yourself to recognise these signals.

- **Target glancing:** The person repeatedly looks at a specific part of your body — your bag, your phone, your jaw — rather than making normal social eye contact. They are assessing you as a target.
- **Grooming gestures:** Nervous energy expressed physically — repeatedly touching the face, adjusting clothing, wiping palms on trousers. This can indicate someone psyching themselves up to act.
- **Hidden hands:** Hands kept out of view — behind the back, tucked in a waistband, inside a jacket. Concealment of the hands is a significant threat indicator.
- **Bladed body posture:** One foot forward, shoulder turned toward you, weight slightly shifted. This is a fighting stance, often adopted subconsciously as a confrontation approaches.
- **Closing distance without cause:** Someone moving toward you in a way that has no social or environmental explanation. They are not walking past — they are walking to you.
- **Inappropriate clothing for conditions:** A heavy jacket on a warm day, or clothing that seems designed to conceal bulk. This can indicate a concealed weapon.
- **Excessive eye contact or deliberate avoidance:** Both can be indicators — either trying to intimidate you, or avoiding your gaze because they intend to act and do not want to be identified.
- **Group dynamics:** Multiple individuals who appear to be together but are separating to surround you, or who are watching a single individual within their group who appears agitated.

## Environmental Scanning

Awareness of your environment is a skill you can build with deliberate, regular practice. Use this process every time you enter a new space.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Identify all exits immediately">
    When you walk into any building, room, vehicle, or outdoor space, locate every exit before you do anything else. Note not just primary exits but secondary ones — fire doors, windows, service corridors. In an emergency, the main entrance may not be accessible.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Note choke points and cover">
    Identify areas where movement would be funnelled and restricted — narrow corridors, stairwells, turnstiles, doorways. Distinguish between *cover* (objects that can stop a bullet or provide genuine protection — engine blocks, concrete pillars) and *concealment* (objects that hide you but offer no ballistic protection — thin walls, car doors). Know which is which before you need to.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Identify who is in the space">
    You do not need to memorise every face. Develop a general sense of how many people are present, where they are positioned, and whether anyone stands out as unusual — by behaviour, demeanour, or positioning rather than by appearance.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Note anything that is out of place">
    Your brain is a powerful pattern-recognition machine. Trust the signal when something feels wrong even if you cannot immediately articulate why. An unattended bag, a door propped open that should be closed, someone dressed inappropriately for the environment — these anomalies deserve a second look.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Position yourself with sightlines and exit access">
    Whenever you have a choice, sit or stand with your back to a solid wall and a clear view of the main approaches to your location. In a restaurant, face the entrance. In a waiting room, choose a seat near an exit with a view of the door. This is not paranoia — it is simple positioning that gives you information and options.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Avoiding Predictable Patterns

Awareness extends beyond the immediate moment. Attackers — particularly those who plan their crimes rather than act opportunistically — look for predictability. Varying your routines removes one of the most significant tools a predator uses.

Take different routes to work, the gym, or regular appointments on a rotating basis. Vary the times you leave and arrive when possible. Avoid the habit of checking your phone the moment you step outside a building — the first few seconds outdoors are when you are most vulnerable and least aware. Look up. Assess.

In parking areas, have your keys ready before you reach your vehicle and approach with a scan of the surrounding area. Check the back seat before entering. On public transport, sit near the driver or guard when possible, keep bags on your lap, and sit with your back to the side of the vehicle rather than facing a stranger directly. In a rideshare, verify the plate, make, model, and driver name before getting in, and share your route with a contact.

## The OODA Loop

Developed by US Air Force Colonel John Boyd, the OODA Loop is a decision-making framework that explains how individuals and organisations respond to rapidly developing threats. Understanding it helps you act faster and more decisively under pressure.

**Observe:** Take in raw information from your environment — what you see, hear, and sense. This is the input stage. The more attuned your situational awareness, the richer and more accurate your observations.

**Orient:** Interpret what you have observed through the lens of your experience, training, and understanding of the situation. This is the most cognitively complex step — it is where mental models built through training pay off. A trained person orients faster because they have seen similar patterns before.

**Decide:** Select a course of action from the options available to you. In a high-stress situation with good awareness and prior training, this step becomes almost automatic — you have already considered your options and pre-selected responses for likely scenarios.

**Act:** Execute your decision immediately and with commitment. Hesitation at this stage is dangerous. Act, then loop back to Observe — because the situation will change in response to your action.

The goal of training your situational awareness is to compress the time it takes to move through Observe → Orient → Decide → Act, so that your response is faster than your attacker's ability to complete their own loop.

<Tip>
  Build your awareness skills in low-stakes, everyday environments. At a coffee shop, challenge yourself to note all exits and describe three other customers without looking again. On a commute, pick a stranger and observe their body language — what are they feeling? What are they paying attention to? These micro-drills build the observational habits that function automatically under stress.
</Tip>
