Shotguns deliver tremendous energy — and with that energy comes substantial recoil. Shooters who fight the gun lose accuracy, slow their follow-up shots, and often develop a flinch that compounds over time. Proper technique transforms that recoil from a problem into a manageable event, keeping your muzzle on target and your body ready to fire again. The fundamentals covered here apply whether you are shooting a pump-action or a semi-automatic.

## Mounting the Shotgun

A correct mount is the single most important physical skill in shotgun shooting. An improperly mounted shotgun delivers recoil directly to bone, causes pain, and makes accurate shooting nearly impossible.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Bring the Stock to Your Shoulder Pocket">
    Place the buttstock firmly into the pocket of your shoulder — the soft, muscular area between your shoulder joint and your collarbone. Never rest the stock on your collarbone. Collarbone contact is the primary cause of shotgun-related bruising and injury.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Set Your Cheek on the Stock">
    Press your cheek firmly against the stock's comb. This is called your cheek weld. It must be consistent on every single shot. Your cheek weld aligns your eye with the sighting system and absorbs part of the rearward recoil impulse before it reaches your face.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Keep Your Head Upright">
    Your head should be upright and neutral — not craned forward or tilted down. If you find yourself ducking your head to reach the stock, the stock may be too short for your length of pull. An adjustable or aftermarket stock can correct this.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Open Both Eyes">
    Train to shoot with both eyes open. This is especially important with a shotgun — your wide-angle situational awareness during a defensive event is critical. With both eyes open, you maintain full peripheral vision while aligning on your target.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Grip the Forend Firmly with Your Support Hand">
    Your support hand should grip the forend firmly and pull it rearward, into your shoulder, with moderate tension. This forward tension actively reinforces your shoulder mount and tightens the entire system. A loose support hand results in a loose, inconsistent mount and excessive muzzle rise.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Stance for Shotgun

The correct stance for defensive shotgun shooting is an aggressive, forward-leaning athletic position — not a square, upright stance. Think of it as the stance you would naturally adopt if you were bracing for a push from the front.

Stand with your feet roughly shoulder-width apart, with your dominant foot slightly back. Lean your upper body forward from the hips — a slight but deliberate lean into the gun. This forward lean places your centre of mass ahead of your heels, allowing your body to absorb recoil instead of being driven backward by it. Bent knees keep you mobile and prevent you from locking up under stress.

In a home-defence context, you may be shooting from awkward positions — kneeling, seated, or around a corner. Practise your mount from all of these positions. Your stance changes; the firmness of your mount and the position of your cheek weld do not.

## Sighting Systems

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Bead Sight">
    The bead sight is the standard sighting system on most factory shotguns — a small brass or steel ball mounted at the muzzle. At defensive distances (inside 15 yards), the bead sight is entirely adequate for the kind of fast, instinctive shooting the situation demands.

    **How to use it:** Focus your eyes on the bead and overlay it on your target. At close range, your pattern will be tight enough that precise alignment is less critical than with a rifle. The bead is a point-shooting aid, not a precision instrument.

    **Limitation:** At distances beyond 15–20 yards, or when firing slugs, the bead sight's lack of a rear reference point makes precise aiming difficult. If your home-defence scenarios may extend beyond that range, consider an upgrade.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Ghost Ring Sights">
    Ghost ring sights add a rear aperture (the "ghost ring") to complement the front sight post. The large, thin rear ring blurs in your vision and "disappears," letting you focus naturally on the front sight and your target. This system is significantly faster and more accurate than a bead at moderate distances.

    **How to use it:** Place the front post in the centre of the rear ring and align the post with your target. The ring will appear as a faint ghost circle in your peripheral vision — hence the name. At close range, ignore the rear ring entirely and shoot off the front post.

    **Recommendation:** Ghost ring sights are the most practical upgrade you can make to a defensive shotgun. They are available for virtually every popular model and install in minutes.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Red Dot Sights">
    Red dot optics — reflex sights or miniature holographic sights — are increasingly common on defensive shotguns. They project a single illuminated dot onto a lens, allowing you to aim with both eyes open and without aligning front and rear sights.

    **Advantages for home defence:**
    - Faster target acquisition, especially under stress
    - Effective in low-light conditions (use an illuminated dot setting appropriate to ambient light)
    - Highly precise at any defensive or extended range when firing slugs
    - No parallax issues within typical defensive distances

    **Considerations:** A quality shotgun-rated red dot (Trijicon MRO, Aimpoint Micro, Holosun 510C) must be mounted on a rail designed to handle shotgun recoil. Standard pistol red dots and many budget options will not survive extended shotgun use. Confirm your optic is rated for shotgun recoil before trusting it for defence.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Recoil Management

Managing shotgun recoil is a matter of mechanics and preparation, not strength. Use your body weight rather than muscle tension to absorb the impulse. Your forward-leaning stance, firm shoulder mount, and pulling support hand work together to channel recoil straight back through your body instead of rotating the muzzle upward.

Focus on maintaining your cheek weld through the shot — do not lift your head in anticipation of the recoil. Lifting your head causes muzzle rise, disrupts your sight picture, and forces a complete re-mount for your next shot. Stay on the gun through the entire recoil cycle by keeping your eye behind the sight.

The term "ride the recoil" describes the technique of staying physically attached to the gun as it moves rearward, then allowing it to return to battery naturally. Stiff arms and a rigid upper body fight the recoil and amplify discomfort. A slightly relaxed but firmly connected upper body absorbs the impulse and returns you to target faster.

<Tip>
  If you are new to shotguns or introducing someone else to the platform, start with reduced-recoil 12-gauge loads or a 20-gauge. Developing a flinch from excessive recoil in early training is far harder to correct than it is to prevent. Build confidence and correct technique with manageable loads first, then step up to full-power defensive ammunition once the fundamentals are solid.
</Tip>

## Pump-Action Manipulation

The pump-action's reliability comes with a requirement: you must cycle it correctly, every time. A properly performed pump stroke is short, violent, and complete. A hesitant or partial stroke causes the most common pump-action malfunction — the short-stroke — which leaves a partially chambered or improperly extracted round and puts you out of the fight.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Fire">
    Pull the trigger through the shot. Maintain your cheek weld and keep your support hand on the forend through the shot.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Press Rearward with Conviction">
    Immediately after the shot, press the trigger group firmly rearward (many pump-actions have a trigger-group release you must depress). Commit to the motion — hesitation causes short-strokes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Full Rearward Stroke">
    Drive the forend fully rearward in one decisive motion until it stops. "Full" means all the way — you should feel or hear the spent case eject and the shell carrier rise. Do not stop halfway.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Full Forward Stroke Until Locked">
    Drive the forend fully forward until it locks into battery. "Locked" means the forend will not move forward any further and the action is fully closed. A round is now chambered and the firearm is ready to fire.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Reacquire Sight Picture and Fire">
    Return to your sight picture immediately after completing the stroke. Under stress, you will be tempted to look at the gun — resist this. Trust the mechanical process and keep your eyes on the threat.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
  A short-stroke occurs when the forend is not cycled completely in either direction. The result is a partially fed, jammed round that can be difficult to clear under stress. The correction is to re-stroke firmly and completely — but prevention is far better. Practice your pump stroke at the range until a full, decisive stroke is automatic muscle memory. Deliberately practice "riding the pump" at reduced speed to ingrain the complete motion before building speed.
</Warning>

## Reloading

### Single-Load Reload (Emergency)

<Steps>
  <Step title="Recognise the Empty Gun">
    The trigger goes dead (nothing happens when you pull it) or you hear the bolt lock back on an empty magazine. Do not freeze — begin the reload immediately.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Take the Shotgun Off Target Briefly">
    Index a round from your side saddle, shell bandolier, or pocket. Keep your eyes on the threat.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Feed One Round Directly into the Chamber">
    Open the ejection port (rack the forend if the bolt is not already back), insert the round directly into the chamber, and close the action. You now have one round ready — fire it if necessary, then continue reloading.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### Tactical Reload (Topping Off the Tube)

<Steps>
  <Step title="Identify a Lull in the Engagement">
    Any pause in the fight — cover is established, the threat has momentarily retreated — is an opportunity to reload. Do not wait for an empty gun if you can avoid it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Keep the Muzzle Downrange">
    Maintain a downrange muzzle orientation and keep your eyes toward the threat while feeding rounds into the magazine tube.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Feed Rounds One at a Time into the Magazine Tube">
    Insert rounds into the magazine tube through the loading gate on the underside of the receiver, one at a time, with your support hand. Load as many rounds as the situation permits.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Return to a Ready Position">
    When the threat re-engages or you have loaded as many rounds as possible, return immediately to your firing grip and fighting stance.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Patterning Your Shotgun

Never assume you know where your shotgun's pattern will land or how wide it will spread. Every combination of barrel, choke, and ammunition produces a different pattern — and the difference between a tight 4-inch cluster and a wide 12-inch spread at 15 yards is the difference between hitting a threat and missing it entirely.

Take your defensive load to the range and fire at paper targets at 7, 10, and 15 yards — the distances most representative of a typical home's interior. Note the pattern centre relative to your point of aim and the pattern diameter at each distance. If your pattern is centred to the left of your point of aim, adjust your technique or have your sights verified. If the pattern is dangerously wide at 10 yards, consider a tighter choke or a different load.

Patterning is not optional — it is the only way to know with certainty what your shotgun will do when you pull the trigger under stress.
