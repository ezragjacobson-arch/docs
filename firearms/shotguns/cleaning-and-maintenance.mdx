Shotguns foul quickly with heavy use, but they are also among the simplest firearms to clean. Powder residue, plastic wad deposits, and unburned propellant accumulate in the bore, chamber, and action with every round fired — and in a semi-automatic shotgun, in the gas system as well. A clean shotgun feeds and cycles reliably when you need it most. Set aside time for a thorough cleaning after every range session and after any period of storage in a humid environment.

## Supplies

Gather these items before you begin:

- Bore brush sized to your gauge (12-gauge or 20-gauge)
- Cleaning rod with patch holder and slotted tip
- Cleaning patches (appropriately sized for your gauge)
- Nylon utility brush (old toothbrush works well)
- Dedicated toothbrush for tight spaces
- Firearms solvent (Hoppes No. 9 or equivalent)
- CLP (cleaner/lubricant/protectant — Ballistol, Break-Free CLP, or equivalent)
- Microfibre cloth for final wipe-down
- Cotton swabs for detail work
- Nitrile gloves (solvent contact is hard on skin)

## Safety Check

<Warning>
  Before touching any part of your shotgun for cleaning, perform a complete unload and clear procedure:

  1. Point the muzzle in a safe direction.
  2. Put the safety on (if applicable).
  3. Open the action completely.
  4. Remove all shells from the magazine tube by cycling the action until it is empty.
  5. Visually and physically inspect the chamber — insert your finger to confirm it is empty.
  6. Visually inspect the magazine tube — confirm it is empty.
  7. Leave the action open throughout the cleaning process as a visual reminder that the firearm is unloaded.

  Never assume a firearm is unloaded. Verify it yourself, every time, before you begin.
</Warning>

## Disassembly

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Pump-Action">
    Most pump-action shotguns (Mossberg 500/590, Remington 870) disassemble without tools for routine cleaning:

    1. **Remove the barrel:** Unscrew the magazine cap at the front of the magazine tube. Pull the forend forward and slide the barrel out of the receiver.
    2. **Slide off the forend:** With the barrel removed, slide the forend assembly (action bars and forend) forward off the magazine tube.
    3. **Remove the bolt from the receiver:** On most designs, the bolt can be slid rearward and out of the receiver once the barrel is removed. Consult your owner's manual for your specific model.
    4. **Remove the trigger group (optional):** On the Mossberg 500/590, a single pin retains the trigger group. Drive it out from left to right with a punch to remove the entire group for deep cleaning. This step is optional for routine maintenance.

    You now have the major components separated for cleaning: barrel, receiver/action, bolt, and forend assembly.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Semi-Automatic">
    Semi-automatic shotgun disassembly varies significantly by design. Always consult your owner's manual before disassembling a semi-automatic shotgun for the first time. Two common systems:

    **Benelli Inertia-Driven System (M2, M4, Super Nova):**
    1. Unscrew the magazine cap and remove it.
    2. Pull the barrel forward off the receiver.
    3. The bolt assembly can be removed rearward out of the receiver.
    4. The inertia spring is housed in the buttstock — no gas system components to clean.

    **Gas-Operated Systems (Mossberg 930, Beretta A300/A400):**
    1. Unscrew the magazine cap.
    2. Remove the barrel.
    3. Remove the gas piston/ring assembly from the magazine tube (varies by model).
    4. Slide out the bolt carrier group.

    Gas-operated designs have more components and require more thorough cleaning of the gas system — see the **Gas System** section below. If you are unsure of any step, stop and consult the manual rather than forcing components.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Cleaning the Bore

<Steps>
  <Step title="Apply Solvent to the Bore Brush">
    Wet your gauge-appropriate bore brush with firearms solvent. Do not oversaturate — a moderate application is sufficient.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Scrub the Bore">
    Run the bore brush through the barrel from the chamber end toward the muzzle if possible (this keeps fouling from being pushed back into the action). Make 8–10 full passes, scrubbing the entire length of the bore. The brush should emerge at the muzzle — do not reverse direction while the brush is inside the barrel.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Let the Solvent Dwell">
    Set the barrel aside for 3–5 minutes to allow the solvent to penetrate and loosen fouling, plastic wad residue, and lead deposits.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Run Dry Patches">
    Attach a patch holder and run dry patches through the bore until they come out clean. Expect 4–8 patches for a heavily used barrel. Patches emerging black or grey indicate significant fouling — re-apply solvent and repeat the brush step.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply a Light Protective Oil Coat">
    Run a final patch lightly dampened with CLP through the bore to leave a thin protective film. A single pass is sufficient. Excess oil in the bore will burn off when you fire, but it can temporarily affect your pattern at the range.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Clean the Chamber">
    Use your bore brush or a chamber brush to scrub the chamber area. Plastic wad residue accumulates heavily in the chamber and just ahead of it — a clean chamber is essential for reliable feeding and extraction.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Cleaning the Action

With the barrel removed and the bolt separated, clean the receiver and action components:

Use your nylon brush or toothbrush with a small amount of solvent to scrub the receiver's interior, paying particular attention to:

- **Bolt face and extractor groove** — carbon and primer residue build up here; scrub until the metal is clean
- **Carrier/lifter** — the shell carrier picks up significant powder fouling; scrub both sides
- **Magazine tube** — wipe the exterior of the tube and the interior if accessible; accumulated grime here can slow feeding
- **Action bars (pump-action)** — wipe the action bars clean with a solvent-dampened cloth; light oil on reassembly

Follow the scrub with a wipe-down using clean patches or a microfibre cloth to remove loosened debris. Do not leave pooled solvent inside the receiver — wipe it dry before lubricating.

## Gas System (Semi-Auto Only)

<Note>
  Gas-operated semi-automatic shotguns require periodic gas system cleaning that is not necessary on pump-action or inertia-driven firearms. Carbon fouling accumulates in the gas ports, gas cylinder, and on the gas piston with every round fired. A fouled gas system will cause failures to cycle — most commonly failures to eject or to feed a fresh round.

  **How often:** Clean the gas system every 250–300 rounds or at any sign of cycling issues.

  **Procedure:**
  1. Remove the gas piston/ring assembly per your owner's manual.
  2. Soak the piston and ring in solvent for 5–10 minutes.
  3. Use a bronze brush to scrub carbon from all surfaces of the piston, piston ring, and gas cylinder bore.
  4. Clear the gas ports (small holes in the magazine tube or barrel) using a cotton swab or a gas port pick — do not enlarge the ports with a drill or pick.
  5. Wipe all components dry and apply a very light coat of CLP before reassembly.

  Consult your manufacturer's manual for the specific gas system design on your firearm. Benelli inertia-driven shotguns do not have a gas system and skip this step entirely.
</Note>

## Lubrication

Apply lubrication sparingly. More is not better — excess oil attracts grit, dust, and unburned powder, and can cause malfunctions in a firearm stored ready for use.

Key lubrication points:

- **Action bars (pump-action):** A light wipe of CLP on both action bars
- **Bolt rails:** Thin film on the rails where the bolt slides in the receiver
- **Bolt body:** Light film on the exterior surfaces
- **Chamber mouth:** Very light oil at the chamber throat to assist feeding
- **Hinge pin and locking surfaces:** A drop of oil at each pivot and bearing surface

<Tip>
  In field conditions — hunting, outdoor training in dusty or wet environments — use less lubrication than you would for range use, not more. A dry-lubed or very lightly oiled action resists grit accumulation far better than a wet one. For a home-defence shotgun stored indoors in a controlled environment, a slightly more generous application is appropriate.
</Tip>

## Reassembly and Function Check

<Steps>
  <Step title="Reassemble in Reverse Order">
    Reinstall all components in the reverse order of disassembly. Refer to your owner's manual if you are uncertain about the orientation of any part. Do not force components — if something resists, stop and confirm correct alignment.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Confirm the Action Cycles Smoothly">
    With the firearm unloaded, cycle the action several times. A pump-action should feel smooth and positive throughout the full stroke. A semi-automatic bolt carrier should slide freely with no binding. Stiffness or grinding indicates a debris particle or an incorrectly assembled component.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Perform a Dummy-Round Function Check">
    Use snap caps or dummy rounds (not live ammunition) to confirm reliable feeding, chambering, and extraction. Load 2–3 dummy rounds into the magazine tube and cycle the action completely. Each dummy round should feed, chamber, and eject cleanly without hesitation.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check the Trigger and Safety">
    With dummy rounds, confirm the trigger functions correctly (fires when you intend, does not fire when safety is engaged) and that the safety engages and disengages positively.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Final Wipe-Down">
    Wipe the exterior of the entire shotgun with a lightly oiled microfibre cloth to remove fingerprints and apply a light protective film. Return the firearm to its storage location.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Maintenance Schedule

| Interval | Task |
|---|---|
| **After each use** | Full bore cleaning, action wipe-down, light lubrication, function check with dummy rounds |
| **Every 250 rounds** | All of the above, plus gas system cleaning (semi-autos), detailed bolt disassembly and cleaning, inspection of extractor and ejector for wear or damage |
| **Annually** | All of the above, plus professional inspection recommended for semi-automatic designs; check stock screws for tightness; inspect barrel for dents, bulges, or crown damage; verify all sights are secure |
| **After exposure to rain/humidity** | Immediate disassembly and thorough drying; inspect for any surface rust; re-lubricate before storage |
