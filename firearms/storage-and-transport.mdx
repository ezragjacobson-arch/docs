Proper storage prevents accidents, keeps firearms out of unauthorised hands, and keeps you compliant with the law. Safe transport ensures you never inadvertently commit an offence simply by moving your firearm from one location to another. Neither storage nor transport is an area where improvisation is acceptable — the consequences of getting it wrong range from tragedy to criminal prosecution. This page gives you the frameworks you need to handle both responsibly.

## Home Storage Options

Your choice of storage solution should balance security against unauthorised access, speed of access in an emergency, and compliance with any storage requirements mandated by your jurisdiction. The four most common options are outlined below.

<Accordion title="Gun Safes">
  A full-size gun safe is the gold standard for home firearm storage. A quality safe protects against theft, prevents access by children and unauthorised adults, and — in models with a fire rating — can protect your firearms and associated documents in the event of a house fire.

  **Recommended features to look for:**
  - **Pry resistance:** Look for a safe with a minimum 10-gauge steel body and reinforced door edges. Bolt thickness of at least 1 inch and a minimum of four locking bolts significantly increases resistance to forced entry.
  - **Fire rating:** A 30-minute or 60-minute fire rating at 1,200°F (650°C) is a reasonable baseline. Higher ratings provide greater protection in a prolonged fire event.
  - **Anchor points:** The safe must be bolted to a structural wall or floor. An unanchored safe — regardless of its weight — can be tipped, rolled, or removed by determined thieves. Pre-drilled anchor holes and anchor hardware should be included or available.
  - **Locking mechanism:** Electronic keypads offer fast access; mechanical dial locks offer greater long-term reliability. Biometric locks are convenient but can fail under stress or with dirty hands.

  A gun safe is a long-term investment. Buy the largest safe you can reasonably afford and accommodate — owners consistently report wishing they had purchased more capacity.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Lock Boxes">
  A lock box (or handgun safe) is a compact, portable steel box designed to secure a single firearm or a small number of handguns. Lock boxes are commonly used at the bedside for a defensive firearm that needs to be accessible quickly, or in a vehicle to secure a firearm while you are away from it.

  **Biometric vs. key access:**
  - **Biometric lock boxes** use fingerprint recognition to allow rapid, key-free access in the dark. They are convenient but can have failure rates under stress, with wet or dirty fingers, or if the sensor is not regularly updated with your fingerprint profile.
  - **Key-access lock boxes** are highly reliable but require you to locate and use a key under stress — which can be slow and difficult in a high-adrenaline situation.
  - **Combination/keypad lock boxes** offer a middle ground — no key required, reasonable speed, and better reliability than biometrics.

  Ensure any lock box is anchored to a fixed structure using its cable or mounting hardware. An unsecured lock box is easily removed and defeated at leisure.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Cable Locks">
  A cable lock threads through the action of a firearm — through the barrel or ejection port — and prevents the action from being closed and the firearm from being fired. Most new firearms in the United States are sold with a cable lock, and many jurisdictions accept them as a legal storage requirement.

  Cable locks are the minimum acceptable level of security and should not be considered a substitute for a proper storage solution. They do not protect against theft, and a determined individual with bolt cutters can defeat most cable locks in seconds.

  **When cable locks are appropriate:**
  - As a supplementary measure inside a locked safe or room
  - For firearms in long-term storage that are not part of a defensive plan
  - When travelling and no other option is available temporarily

  Do not rely on a cable lock alone to prevent child access or theft.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Trigger Locks">
  A trigger lock is a clamshell device that fits around the trigger guard and prevents the trigger from being operated. Like cable locks, they are widely sold as entry-level safety devices and are accepted as legal minimum compliance in some jurisdictions.

  Trigger locks share the same limitations as cable locks — they prevent the trigger from being operated but do not prevent the firearm from being stolen or removed from the home. They should be treated as a last resort and used in conjunction with other security measures, such as storage in a locked room or cabinet.

  **Important:** Never attempt to apply or remove a trigger lock to or from a loaded firearm. Always confirm the firearm is unloaded before using a trigger lock.
</Accordion>

## Recommended Storage Practices

Beyond choosing the right hardware, consistent habits are what make your storage system genuinely safe.

- **Store ammunition separately from firearms.** Keeping your firearms and ammunition in different locations adds a meaningful obstacle to misuse by an unauthorised person who accesses one but not the other.
- **Keep all firearms unloaded when stored**, with the exception of a designated home defence firearm secured in a quick-access safe. Storing loaded firearms in unsecured locations is dangerous and illegal in many jurisdictions.
- **Secure every firearm from children and unauthorised users.** Assume no lock, latch, or high shelf is sufficient on its own — children are persistent and often more capable of defeating improvised barriers than adults expect. Use rated, anchored, dedicated security hardware.
- **Use a safe with a solid anchor point.** A safe that cannot be moved cannot be removed. Anchor to floor joists or structural wall studs, not drywall alone.
- **Conduct periodic inventory.** Know exactly what firearms and how much ammunition you have in storage, and check periodically that your count is correct and your storage hardware is functioning properly.
- **Educate household members appropriately.** Adults in the household should know where firearms are stored, how to access them in an emergency, and the safety rules that govern their handling. Children should receive age-appropriate safety education — programmes such as the NRA's Eddie Eagle curriculum are designed for this purpose.

## Quick-Access Safes for Home Defence

A home defence firearm is only useful if you can access it rapidly under stress. A full-size gun safe requiring a dial combination is not practical for a bedside defensive handgun. Quick-access safes are designed to solve this problem — they provide meaningful security against casual access while allowing a trained adult to retrieve the firearm in seconds.

Modern quick-access safes use biometric fingerprint sensors, RFID tags (accessed by a bracelet or card worn near the bed), or illuminated electronic keypads. Each has trade-offs: biometric sensors are fastest but most prone to failure in adverse conditions; RFID is reliable but requires you to have the access device on your person; keypads are highly reliable and can be operated in the dark with practice.

Position your quick-access safe where you can reach it from your sleeping position without sitting up if possible, and anchor it firmly — either to the bedframe, nightstand, or wall. Practice accessing it in low-light conditions until the motion is automatic. The goal is to be able to access your firearm quickly, safely, and without fumbling, even when woken suddenly.

Balancing quick adult access against child safety is the central challenge. A quick-access safe properly anchored and rated is designed precisely for this — it is meaningfully more secure than a drawer, a box on a shelf, or a trigger lock, while still being accessible to an authorised adult in seconds.

## Transporting Firearms

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="In Your Vehicle">
    **General principles:**

    Transporting a firearm in a vehicle requires careful compliance with the laws of every jurisdiction you pass through — not just where you start and where you end up. Laws vary widely; what follows is a widely recommended best practice that aligns with the laws of many jurisdictions, but it is not a substitute for researching the specific rules that apply to you.

    **Best practice for vehicle transport:**
    1. The firearm should be **unloaded** — no round in the chamber, magazine removed or empty.
    2. Transport in a **locked, hard-sided case**. Soft cases do not satisfy the legal definition of "locked container" in most jurisdictions. The lock should require a key or combination to open.
    3. Store the case in the **boot (trunk)** of the vehicle, physically separated from the passenger compartment. In vehicles without a separate boot (SUVs, hatchbacks, trucks), a locked case in the rear cargo area satisfies most jurisdictions' requirements.
    4. Store **ammunition separately** from the firearm — in a separate case or bag.
    5. If you hold a concealed carry permit, understand whether it applies in your vehicle and whether specific rules govern how the firearm must be carried when driving.

    **Jurisdiction-specific issues to research:**
    - State and provincial laws regarding transport of loaded vs. unloaded firearms
    - Rules governing transport through jurisdictions where you do not hold a carry permit
    - Specific rules for hollowpoint ammunition transport in some states
    - Requirements to inform law enforcement of the presence of a firearm during a traffic stop
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Air Travel">
    **Travelling by commercial aircraft with a firearm is legal in most countries but is strictly regulated.** Failure to comply with airline and regulatory requirements can result in significant fines, confiscation of your firearm, and criminal charges.

    **Standard requirements (always verify with your airline and the applicable authority before travelling):**

    1. The firearm must be **unloaded** — no round in the chamber, and the magazine removed from the firearm.
    2. Transport in a **locked, hard-sided case** that cannot be pried open easily. The lock must be a non-TSA lock — only you should hold the key or combination, not a master key held by the airline or TSA.
    3. The case must be **checked as baggage** — firearms are never permitted in carry-on baggage under any circumstances.
    4. You must **declare the firearm** at the check-in counter before the bag is accepted. The agent will give you a declaration card to place inside the case. Failure to declare is a serious offence.
    5. **Ammunition** must also be in checked baggage, in its original factory packaging or in a case designed for ammunition. Most airlines cap the quantity of ammunition you may transport — check the specific limit for your carrier.
    6. Comply with **TSA rules** (in the United States) and the equivalent authority in your country, as well as the specific policies of your airline — airlines may impose stricter rules than the minimum regulatory requirement.

    **International travel** introduces additional layers of complexity, including import and export permits, destination-country firearms laws, and airline policies that may prohibit firearms transport entirely on certain routes. Research each country's laws thoroughly before attempting to transport a firearm internationally.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Legal Compliance

<Warning>
  Transport laws for firearms vary enormously between countries, states, provinces, and municipalities. A transport configuration that is fully legal in your home state may constitute a serious criminal offence the moment you cross a border. Before transporting a firearm anywhere — by vehicle, by air, or by any other means — research the specific laws of every jurisdiction you will enter. Pay particular attention to rules governing loaded vs. unloaded transport, case requirements, permit reciprocity, and notification obligations. If you are in any doubt, consult a qualified attorney who specialises in firearms law in the relevant jurisdiction. Do not rely on advice from friends, online forums, or general summaries — including the guidance on this page — as a substitute for jurisdiction-specific legal research.
</Warning>

<Tip>
  Consider completing a certified firearm storage and safety course offered by a recognised organisation such as the NRA, USCCA, or their national equivalents in your country. These courses cover safe storage practices, legal requirements, accident prevention, and handling emergencies involving firearms in the home — and many jurisdictions accept completion as evidence of compliance with safe-storage requirements. Find a course near you through the NRA's course finder, the USCCA's training locator, or your country's national firearms safety body.
</Tip>
