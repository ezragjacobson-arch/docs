Accurate, reliable shooting starts with fundamentals. Even under stress, shooters who have drilled proper technique perform far better than those who rely on natural instinct alone. The body's stress response — tunnel vision, loss of fine motor skills, elevated heart rate — will degrade technique, which means your baseline must be high enough to remain effective when it counts. Build each skill deliberately, in order, before combining them at speed.

## Stance

Your stance creates the foundation for everything else. A stable, replicable stance lets you manage recoil, track your sights, and fire fast follow-up shots.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Isosceles">
    The isosceles stance is the most widely taught modern defensive shooting stance and the one most naturally adopted under stress.

    - Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart, one foot very slightly ahead of the other
    - Face the target squarely — your chest is perpendicular to the firing line
    - Bend your knees slightly and shift your weight forward onto the balls of your feet
    - Extend both arms fully toward the target, forming a triangle (isosceles) shape when viewed from above
    - Keep your shoulders forward and down — do not shrug

    **Advantages:** Naturally assumed under stress, provides good recoil management with both arms equally extended, allows fast lateral movement, works well with body armour.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Weaver">
    The Weaver stance was developed by Los Angeles County Deputy Sheriff Jack Weaver in the late 1950s and dominated competitive and law enforcement shooting for decades.

    - Stand in a fighting stance with your non-dominant foot forward
    - Your body is bladed approximately 45 degrees to the target
    - Your strong arm extends toward the target while your support arm bends at the elbow, pulling the pistol back toward you
    - The push-pull tension between the two arms controls recoil and steadies the pistol
    - Your head turns to face the target, which means you are looking over your shoulder slightly

    **Advantages:** Reduces your body profile as a target, excellent recoil control through the push-pull mechanism, comfortable for shooters with a strong-eye dominant stance.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Chapman / Modified Weaver">
    The Chapman stance (also called the Modified Weaver) splits the difference between the two above and is popular among defensive shooting instructors.

    - Adopt a bladed stance similar to the Weaver, but less extreme — approximately 30 degrees off the target
    - Extend your strong arm fully (like the Isosceles) while your support arm remains bent (like the Weaver)
    - Your support elbow points toward the ground rather than out to the side
    - Weight shifts slightly forward onto your non-dominant foot

    **Advantages:** Combines the stability of a fully extended strong arm with the recoil-absorbing bend of the Weaver support arm. Many shooters find it more natural than a pure Weaver while maintaining better target coverage than a pure Isosceles.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Grip

A proper grip is the single most important physical fundamental in handgun shooting. A poor grip causes malfunctions, poor accuracy, and slow follow-up shots. Build yours step by step.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Establish a High Grip">
    Place the web of your strong hand as high as possible on the pistol's backstrap. The barrel bore axis should be as close to the centerline of your forearm as the frame allows. A high grip reduces muzzle flip and gives you better control during recoil.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Wrap Your Fingers Around the Grip">
    Curl your three lower fingers firmly around the front strap of the grip. Your index finger stays off the trigger and outside the trigger guard, indexed straight along the frame. Apply firm, even pressure — grip as hard as you can without causing your hands to shake.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Position Your Support Hand">
    Rotate your support hand so the palm faces the pistol. Fill the exposed portion of the grip with the meaty pad of your support palm, pressing it flat against the side of the grip. There should be no gap between your support palm and the grip panels.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Stack Your Thumbs Forward">
    Both thumbs point forward toward the target, stacked on the same side of the frame (strong thumb on top for most shooters). Your thumbs should not cross — crossing thumbs behind a semi-automatic's slide can cause a painful cut when the slide cycles.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply the 60/40 Pressure Rule">
    Approximately 60% of your total grip pressure comes from the support hand, and 40% from the strong hand. This distribution frees up your index finger to operate the trigger smoothly without disturbing the gun's alignment. Squeeze the pistol front-to-back with both hands simultaneously — never side-to-side.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Note>
  Limp-wristing is one of the most common causes of semi-automatic pistol malfunctions, particularly stovepipes and failures to feed. When your wrists flex under recoil instead of staying firm, you rob the slide of the resistance it needs to complete its full cycle. If you experience repeated malfunctions that disappear when a more experienced shooter fires the same pistol, focus on firming up your grip and locking your wrists.
</Note>

## Sight Alignment and Sight Picture

Most handguns use a three-part iron sight system: a front sight (a post or blade) and a rear sight (a notch). Sight alignment means centering the front sight in the rear notch so that the tops of both sights are level. Sight picture means placing that aligned sight system on the correct part of your target.

The relationship between the three elements — rear sight, front sight, and target — is called the **sight plane**. Because your eye can only focus sharply at one distance at a time, you must decide where to place your focus. In defensive shooting, you focus sharply on the front sight. The rear sight and target will both appear slightly blurry. This is correct and intentional. A sharp front sight on a blurry target is far more accurate than a sharp target with vague sights.

For defensive shooting at typical engagement distances (3–15 yards), a consistent, repeatable sight picture on the center of mass is your goal.

<Tip>
  Train yourself to focus on the front sight, not the target. Your brain naturally wants to look at the threat, but your shots will land where your front sight is pointing — not where your eyes are looking. During dry-fire practice, consciously say "front sight" to yourself as you press the trigger. This verbal cue builds the habit faster than repetition alone.
</Tip>

## Trigger Control

Trigger control is how you fire the shot without disturbing the sight picture you have established. Press the trigger straight to the rear using the pad of your index finger — the center of the fingertip, between the tip and the first joint. Do not use the crease of the first joint (too far in) or the very tip (too far out).

Press the trigger smoothly and continuously with increasing pressure until the shot breaks. Avoid anticipating the shot — any muscular contraction in anticipation of recoil (also called flinching) will drive the muzzle down or to the side before the bullet has left the barrel. Anticipation is the primary cause of shots landing low in right-handed shooters.

After the shot, maintain contact with the trigger as it moves forward. Allow it to reset — you will feel and possibly hear a distinct click — then stop. Do not release the trigger fully to the forward wall before beginning the next press. A short reset gives you faster, more controlled follow-up shots.

<Accordion title="Dry-Fire Practice Drills for Trigger Control">
  Dry-fire practice (with an unloaded, verified-clear firearm) is the most efficient way to build trigger control. It costs nothing and can be done at home.

  **The Coin Drill**
  Balance a coin on the front sight of your pistol. Press the trigger through to the break. If the coin falls, you disturbed the muzzle — identify whether it was your trigger finger, your grip, or anticipation, and correct it. Repeat until you can fire 10 consecutive dry shots without dropping the coin.

  **The Wall Drill**
  Stand close to a blank wall and press your muzzle against it. Press the trigger — if the muzzle moves, the wall will tell you instantly. This drill isolates trigger movement from all other variables.

  **The Surprise Break Drill**
  Have a training partner randomly insert a dummy round among live rounds during range practice. When the hammer falls on a dummy round, your reaction (or lack thereof) reveals exactly how much you are anticipating the shot.

  Always triple-check that your firearm is unloaded before any dry-fire session, and never dry-fire in a room where live ammunition is present.
</Accordion>

## Breathing

Breathing causes your body — and your sights — to rise and fall rhythmically. At defensive distances this movement is less critical, but at any distance beyond 15 yards, uncontrolled breathing visibly walks your shots off target.

Use the respiratory pause technique: take a normal breath, exhale approximately half of it, then pause your breathing naturally and press the trigger during that pause. Do not hold a full breath — the resulting oxygen deprivation and physical tension will increase your heartbeat and cause tremors. The pause should last no more than 6–8 seconds. If you need more time, breathe and start again.

## Follow-Through

<Note>
  Follow-through means maintaining your grip, trigger position, and sight alignment through the moment of ignition and until the slide returns to battery. Many shooters relax their grip, lower the pistol, or break their sight picture the instant they hear the shot — often before the bullet has actually left the barrel. This is a habit built from an unconscious anticipation of the shot being "over." During both dry-fire and live-fire practice, call your shot (identify exactly where your sights were when the gun fired) and stay on target through the trigger reset. Good follow-through turns one accurate shot into a platform for a faster, equally accurate second shot.
</Note>

## Malfunction Clearance

Malfunctions happen. Train to clear them without looking down at the gun and without breaking your visual contact with the threat.

**Type 1 Malfunction — Failure to Fire (Tap, Rack, Assess)**

<Steps>
  <Step title="Tap">
    Strike the base of the magazine firmly with the heel of your support hand to ensure it is fully seated. A loose magazine is one of the most common causes of failures to feed and fire.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Rack">
    Grasp the slide with your support hand (overhand or slingshot grip) and rack it vigorously to the rear and release it. This ejects any faulty or partially chambered round and chambers a fresh one from the magazine.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Assess">
    Return to your sight picture, assess the threat, and fire if necessary. If the pistol still does not fire, transition to a Type 3 clearance or draw a backup weapon.
  </Step>
</Steps>

**Type 3 Malfunction — Double Feed**

A double feed occurs when two rounds attempt to enter the chamber simultaneously. The slide will be partially out of battery and the trigger will not function. Tap-Rack will not clear it.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Lock the Slide to the Rear">
    While keeping your muzzle on the threat, lock the slide back using the slide stop lever.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Rip Out the Magazine">
    Strip the magazine from the pistol with force. It may be under tension from the rounds jammed in the action. Retain or discard the magazine depending on your situation.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Rack Three Times">
    Rack the slide three times to clear any remaining rounds or casings from the chamber and ejection port.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Insert a Fresh Magazine">
    Seat a fresh magazine firmly until it locks.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Rack and Assess">
    Rack the slide once to chamber a round. Assess the threat and resume firing if necessary.
  </Step>
</Steps>
