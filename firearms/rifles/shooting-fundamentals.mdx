Rifles reward good technique with exceptional accuracy, but bad fundamentals magnify errors over distance. Whether you are shooting at 25 yards indoors or 200 yards outdoors, the same principles apply — position, grip, cheek weld, sight picture, trigger control, and follow-through all compound on each other. A 1-millimetre flaw at the muzzle becomes several inches of error on target at distance. Build your fundamentals methodically, and the platform will do the rest.

## Shooting Positions

Your choice of position determines how stable your shooting platform is, how quickly you can move, and how much of your body you expose. Match the position to the situation.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Prone">
    The prone position is the most stable shooting platform available without a bench or bipod. By placing your entire body on the ground, you lower your centre of gravity to its minimum, create maximum contact with a stable surface, and minimise your target profile.

    **How to assume the prone position**
    - Lie face-down with your body at a slight angle to the target (not directly behind the rifle — this forces your spine to absorb recoil)
    - Your strong-side leg can remain straight; bend your support-side knee slightly for stability
    - Both elbows contact the ground, forming a stable triangle with the rifle
    - Position your body so the rifle's natural point of aim (where it sits when your muscles are relaxed) is on target — do not muscle the rifle onto target

    **Advantages:** Maximum stability, lowest recoil felt, smallest target profile, ideal for precision shooting at distance.

    **Limitations:** Slow to enter and exit, impractical on wet or uneven ground, limits field of view and lateral awareness.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Kneeling">
    The kneeling position offers a useful balance between stability and mobility. You present a smaller target than standing while retaining the ability to move quickly.

    **How to assume the kneeling position**
    - Drop onto your strong-side knee; your support-side foot is flat on the ground with the knee directly above it
    - Sit back onto your strong-side heel to lower your centre of gravity
    - Place the flat of your support arm's elbow on top of your support-side knee — do not balance the elbow on the kneecap point, which is unstable; use the flat meaty area just behind it
    - Your strong-side elbow points down and rearward, not out to the side

    **Advantages:** Faster to assume and exit than prone, stable enough for accurate fire out to 100+ yards, allows better lateral movement than prone.

    **Limitations:** Less stable than prone, exposing more body than prone.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Standing (Offhand)">
    The standing or offhand position is the least stable but the fastest to deploy and the most commonly used in dynamic defensive situations. You are fully mobile and have maximum situational awareness.

    **How to assume the standing position**
    - Stand with feet shoulder-width apart, support-side foot slightly forward
    - Bend your knees slightly — a locked knee stance collapses under recoil and fatigue
    - Support hand grips the handguard as far forward as comfortable
    - Pull the stock firmly into the shoulder pocket — the curved area between your shoulder and clavicle
    - Lean slightly forward into the rifle

    **Advantages:** Maximum mobility and situational awareness, quickest to transition from, necessary in confined spaces.

    **Limitations:** Least stable, highest hold error, most reliant on good technique to compensate for body movement.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Braced / Supported">
    Using a barricade, wall corner, vehicle, or bipod as a support drastically improves your stability in any base position. In a defensive or precision context, always use available support.

    **How to use a barricade effectively**
    - Never rest the barrel or handguard directly on a hard surface — this changes the barrel's harmonics and shifts your point of impact
    - Rest your hand on the barricade and the rifle on your hand, or use a bipod or bag
    - When shooting around a corner, expose only what is necessary — keep as much of your body behind cover as possible
    - Match the position to the height of the support: kneel behind a low barricade, stand at a window sill

    **Advantages:** Near-prone stability while remaining vertical, critical for precision shots at distance, significantly reduces wobble in standing position.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Cheek Weld and Eye Relief

Cheek weld is the consistent, firm contact between your cheekbone and the stock of the rifle. It is the anchor point that aligns your eye with your sights or optic, shot after shot. An inconsistent cheek weld is one of the most common causes of unexplained shot-to-shot variation.

To establish your cheek weld, press your cheek firmly onto the stock at the same point every time. The stock should touch your cheek, not the other way around — drive your face down onto the stock actively, do not just let your head float near it. Adjustable cheekpieces on precision stocks allow you to set the height exactly; on fixed stocks, you can use cheek-riser padding.

**Eye relief** is the distance between your eye and the rear of your optic. Most rifle scopes have a defined eye relief range of 3–4 inches. If your eye is too close, you risk scope bite — the ocular end striking your eyebrow or brow ridge under recoil, commonly called "getting scoped." If your eye is too far back, the sight picture blacks out partially or fully. Position your scope so you see a full, clear sight picture with no shadow around the edge when your cheek weld is properly established. Mount the scope where your eye naturally falls, then lock it in place.

## Grip and Support Hand

<Steps>
  <Step title="Strong Hand High on the Pistol Grip">
    Grip the pistol grip with your strong hand so the web between your thumb and index finger sits as high as possible. Your index finger stays outside the trigger guard and indexed along the receiver. Grip firmly but not so tight that your hand shakes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Support Hand Forward on the Handguard">
    Place your support hand as far forward on the handguard as is comfortable and controllable. A forward grip gives you more leverage against muzzle rise and allows you to push the rifle outward while your strong hand pulls it rearward into your shoulder. Vertical foregrips (VFGs) and angled foregrips (AFGs) are accessories that many shooters use to optimise this position.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Pull the Stock Firmly Into Your Shoulder Pocket">
    The stock must be seated firmly in the shoulder pocket — the curved muscular area between your shoulder joint and clavicle. A loose stock allows the rifle to move before and during recoil, breaking your cheek weld and shifting your point of impact. Use your strong-hand grip to actively pull the rifle rearward into your shoulder on every shot.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Keep Your Elbows In">
    Elbows that flare outward weaken the structure of your shooting platform and expose more of your body if shooting around cover. Drive both elbows toward the ground. Your support elbow in particular should point toward the ground, not out to the side, when shooting in the standing position.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Create a Bone-on-Bone Platform">
    In stable positions, align your skeletal structure to support the rifle's weight rather than relying on muscle tension alone. Muscles tire and introduce wobble; bones do not. In prone, shift your position until the rifle sits on target with minimal muscular effort — this is your natural point of aim.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Trigger Control

Rifle trigger control follows the same principles as handgun trigger control but is even more consequential at distance. Press the trigger straight to the rear using the pad of your index finger. Apply smooth, continuous pressure without any abrupt increase in force at the end of the press.

Avoid anticipating the shot. At longer distances, even minor muscular anticipation — a fractional dip of the muzzle, a shift of the support hand — translates into significant error on target. If you find yourself flinching, return to dry-fire practice to isolate and eliminate the habit.

After the shot breaks, do not immediately release the trigger to its forward position. Allow the trigger to move forward only until you feel (and sometimes hear) the reset click, then stop. Begin the next press from the reset point rather than from a fully forward trigger. This short reset is faster and maintains your trigger-finger discipline between shots.

For bolt-action rifles, follow through completely on each shot before working the bolt. Cyclers who operate the bolt the instant they feel recoil often disturb the rifle before the bullet has left the barrel.

## Breathing

At defensive distances of 0–50 yards, breathing control is less critical than at longer ranges, but it remains a good habit to build. Uncontrolled breathing moves your muzzle in a vertical arc with each breath cycle, and your heart rate adds a smaller horizontal oscillation on top of that.

Use the respiratory pause: inhale normally, exhale approximately half of your breath, then pause naturally and press the trigger during the pause. Your body is at its most relaxed and still at the natural respiratory pause point. Do not hold a full breath — the resulting increase in blood pressure and muscle tension is counterproductive. If you need more time, complete the breath cycle and begin again. The pause should not exceed 8–10 seconds.

At distances beyond 100 yards, pair your respiratory pause with a brief period of stillness to let your heartbeat's oscillation settle into a predictable rhythm.

## Using Optics

<Accordion title="Iron Sights">
  Iron sights consist of a front post or bead and a rear aperture (peep sight) or notch. On AR-platform rifles, the standard rear sight is a dual-aperture flip sight — a small aperture for precision shooting and a larger aperture for fast, close-range acquisition.

  To use iron sights correctly, align the front post in the centre of the rear aperture and focus your eye on the front sight. The rear sight and target will appear slightly blurry — this is correct. Iron sights require no batteries, add no weight to the rifle, and cannot lose zero from a battery failure. Every shooter should be competent with iron sights regardless of what optic they run.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Red Dot Sights">
  Red dot sights (RDS) project an illuminated reticle (typically a dot or chevron) onto a lens at a set focal point. They are parallax-free at their rated distance, meaning the dot appears on the target regardless of where in the lens your eye sits. This makes them exceptionally fast for target acquisition at close to medium distances (0–100 yards).

  To use a red dot effectively, keep both eyes open and allow your dominant eye to acquire the dot naturally. Bring the rifle up into your cheek weld and the dot will appear on the target. Dial the dot brightness to match your ambient light conditions — a dot that is too bright will obscure the target; too dim and it disappears in bright light. Popular examples: Aimpoint PRO, Trijicon MRO, Holosun 510C.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Magnified Scopes">
  Fixed or variable magnification scopes are essential for precision shooting beyond 100 yards. A 4x scope triples your ability to resolve fine aiming points compared to iron sights; a 10x scope allows precise holdovers at 500 yards and beyond.

  Critical concepts for scope use:
  - **Eye relief:** Position the scope so you have a full, shadow-free sight picture at your established cheek weld
  - **Parallax adjustment:** At higher magnifications, parallax error becomes meaningful — adjust the parallax dial so the reticle and target are on the same focal plane
  - **Zero:** Set your zero for a specific distance (common choices are 25 yards, 50 yards, or 100 yards depending on calibre and purpose) and know your holdovers or turret adjustments for other distances
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="LPVO (Low Power Variable Optic)">
  LPVOs combine the best features of a red dot and a magnified scope. A true 1x setting (look for "true 1x" rather than "1x zoom") gives you both-eyes-open speed comparable to a red dot. Dialling up to 4x, 6x, or 8x gives you the precision of a magnified scope for longer shots.

  For home defence and general-purpose defensive rifle use, an LPVO on its 1x setting is an extremely capable choice. Set to a higher magnification, the same optic serves for range practice at distance. The trade-off is weight and cost compared to a standalone red dot. Popular examples: Vortex Razor Gen III 1-6x, Nightforce NX8 1-8x, Leupold VX-6HD 1-6x.
</Accordion>

## Malfunction Clearance

Train malfunction drills until they are automatic. In a defensive scenario, you cannot afford to look down at your rifle and diagnose the problem — your response must be a practised reflex.

**Type 1 — Failure to Feed or Fire**

<Steps>
  <Step title="Tap">
    Strike the base of the magazine firmly with the heel of your support hand to ensure it is fully seated. A partially seated magazine is a frequent cause of failures to feed.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Rack">
    Pull the charging handle fully to the rear with your support hand and release it completely. This ejects any misfired or partially chambered round and chambers a fresh one.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Assess and Fire">
    Return to your sight picture. Assess the threat. If the rifle is now functional, fire as necessary.
  </Step>
</Steps>

**Type 2 — Failure to Eject (Stovepipe)**

A stovepipe occurs when an empty case is not fully ejected and is caught in the ejection port by the closing bolt, resembling a stovepipe in silhouette.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Sweep the Case Clear">
    Use your support hand to swipe across the ejection port in a rearward motion, knocking the protruding case free.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Rack">
    Pull the charging handle fully to the rear and release.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Assess and Fire">
    Confirm the action is clear, return to your sight picture, and fire as necessary.
  </Step>
</Steps>

**Type 3 — Double Feed**

A double feed is the most serious common malfunction. Two rounds attempt to enter the chamber simultaneously, locking the bolt partially out of battery. The trigger will not function. Tap-Rack will not clear it.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Lock the Bolt to the Rear">
    Rotate the rifle slightly so the ejection port faces down. Grasp the charging handle and lock the bolt carrier to the rear.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Remove the Magazine">
    Depress the magazine release and rip the magazine out with your support hand. You may need to force it — the rounds jammed in the action can apply significant tension to the magazine.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Rack Three Times">
    Rack the charging handle three times to clear all remaining rounds and cases from the action. Allow the ejection port to face downward so debris falls clear.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Insert a Fresh Magazine">
    Seat a loaded magazine firmly until it locks with an audible click.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Chamber a Round and Assess">
    Release the bolt carrier from the rear using the bolt catch. Confirm the action is in battery. Assess the threat and fire if necessary.
  </Step>
</Steps>
