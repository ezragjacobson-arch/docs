Rifles accumulate carbon fouling quickly, especially in the gas system. Every round fired deposits carbon residue inside the bolt carrier group, along the gas tube, and throughout the action. This fouling increases friction, accelerates wear, and — if ignored long enough — causes malfunctions at the worst possible time. Regular cleaning keeps your rifle cycling reliably, protects the finish and barrel, and extends the service life of critical components. Build a cleaning habit that matches how frequently you shoot.

## Supplies Needed

Collect all of your supplies before you begin disassembly. Working with a partially stripped rifle while searching for equipment increases the risk of mishandling.

- Bore guide (prevents solvent from contaminating the lower receiver and protects the chamber)
- Bore brush (bronze or nylon, specific to your calibre)
- Cleaning rod (one-piece aluminium or carbon fibre preferred; segmented rods risk damaging the crown)
- Pull-through cleaner (e.g., Bore Snake) as a quick-clean alternative
- Cleaning patches (cotton, sized for your calibre)
- Chamber brush (separate from the bore brush; stiffer bristles for the chamber)
- Bolt carrier group (BCG) brush or dedicated carbon scraper
- Carbon scraper tool (for AR-platform BCG and carrier key)
- CLP (Cleaner, Lubricant, Protectant — e.g., Slip 2000, Break-Free CLP)
- Quality gun oil or dedicated lubricant
- Toothbrush and nylon utility brush
- Roll of paper towels or a supply of lint-free rags
- Cleaning mat to protect your work surface and catch solvent

## Safety Check

<Warning>
  Confirm your rifle is completely unloaded before handling it for cleaning. Complete every step below before proceeding.

  1. Point the muzzle in a safe direction.
  2. Remove the magazine. Set it on a separate surface away from the rifle.
  3. Lock the bolt to the rear using the charging handle and bolt catch.
  4. Visually inspect the chamber — look through the ejection port and confirm there is no round present.
  5. Physically inspect the chamber — insert a finger and confirm it is empty.
  6. Keep all live ammunition off the cleaning area entirely.

  Never begin disassembly until you have personally completed all five steps above.
</Warning>

## Field Strip (AR-Platform)

The AR-platform field strips into its major components quickly and without tools, making routine cleaning straightforward.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Remove the Magazine and Clear the Rifle">
    Press the magazine release and remove the magazine. Lock the bolt to the rear, visually and physically inspect the chamber, then allow the bolt to go forward on an empty chamber.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Push Out the Rear Takedown Pin">
    Locate the rear takedown pin on the lower receiver (the rearmost of the two pins). Push it from right to left using a punch, cartridge tip, or your finger until it stops. It will not come fully out — it is captured.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Push Out the Front Pivot Pin">
    Push the front pivot pin (forward of the magazine well) from right to left in the same manner. The upper and lower receivers will now hinge open at the front.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Separate Upper and Lower Receivers">
    Rotate the upper receiver upward away from the lower, then pull it forward and free. Set the lower receiver aside.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Remove the Bolt Carrier Group">
    With the upper receiver in hand, grasp the charging handle and pull it rearward simultaneously with the BCG. Slide the BCG out of the upper receiver. Then slide the charging handle out of its channel.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Separate the Bolt from the Carrier">
    Rotate the cam pin 90 degrees and lift it out. Slide the bolt forward and out of the carrier. Remove the firing pin by tilting the carrier — it will drop out of its recess. Retain the firing pin retaining cotter pin (the small C-shaped clip at the rear of the carrier).
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Cleaning the Bore

A clean bore is critical for accuracy and longevity. Use a bore guide when cleaning from the chamber end to protect the chamber and prevent solvent contamination of the lower receiver.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Insert the Bore Guide and Apply Solvent">
    Insert the bore guide into the chamber of the upper receiver. Apply bore solvent or CLP liberally to your calibre-specific bore brush.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Scrub the Bore">
    Push the brush through the bore from the chamber end to the muzzle, then pull it back. Complete 8–12 full passes. The solvent loosens copper jacket fouling from the rifling grooves and dissolves carbon deposits.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Run Solvent-Soaked Patches">
    Switch to your patch holder and run solvent-soaked patches through the bore one at a time, discarding each after a single pass. Continue until the patches come out visibly cleaner.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Run Dry Patches">
    Follow the solvent patches with dry patches until a patch comes out clean and white (or very lightly grey). All solvent residue should be removed.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply a Light Oil Patch">
    Run a patch with a single drop of gun oil through the bore. This leaves a thin corrosion-protective film. Do not over-oil the bore — excess oil can contribute to fouling buildup and create a bore obstruction if left for extended periods.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Cleaning the Bolt Carrier Group

The BCG is the most carbon-intensive component to clean on an AR-platform rifle. It operates inside the gas stream and accumulates hard carbon rapidly.

Begin by scraping the exterior of the bolt — particularly around the locking lugs, the gas rings, and the extractor. Use a carbon scraper or the back edge of a utility brush to chip off the hard, baked-on carbon that accumulates in these areas. A brass or polymer scraper is safer than steel, which can leave scratches that become fouling-collection points.

Clean the interior of the carrier tube (the hole the bolt moves inside) with a BCG brush saturated in solvent. Scrub the carrier key — the small bridge on top of the carrier that accepts gas from the gas tube — inside and out. Carbon inside the carrier key restricts gas flow and causes failures to cycle.

Use your toothbrush and solvent to clean the cam pin hole and the firing pin channel. Wipe all surfaces dry with a clean patch or cloth before lubricating.

<Note>
  Do not over-lubricate the BCG. A common misconception is that "wet is good" for AR-platform rifles. In reality, excess oil on the BCG mixes with carbon fouling to form a thick, black paste that accelerates fouling accumulation and can eventually impede bolt movement. Apply oil to the BCG lightly — a shiny coat, not pools of oil.
</Note>

## Cleaning the Chamber and Feed Ramp

The chamber and feed ramp are critical surfaces for reliable feeding and extraction. Use your chamber brush — not the bore brush — saturated in solvent to scrub the chamber. Work the brush in a circular motion rather than pushing it through the bore. Scrub the feed ramp in the barrel extension with a toothbrush and solvent.

Wipe the chamber dry with a patch or cloth. A light coat of oil in the chamber is acceptable for storage but wipe it out before shooting — excessive oil in the chamber can cause a case to fail to extract by hydraulically preventing the case from gripping the chamber wall.

## Gas System

<Accordion title="Gas Tube">
  The gas tube runs from the gas block (on the barrel) back to the upper receiver, delivering the gas that cycles the bolt carrier group. On a direct-impingement AR-platform rifle, carbon deposits accumulate inside the gas tube over thousands of rounds.

  For routine maintenance, you do not need to clean the inside of the gas tube. The gas flowing through it at high temperature and pressure self-cleans it to a significant degree. Inspect the tube for dents, kinks, or corrosion at each cleaning session. If you experience persistent short-stroking or failure-to-cycle malfunctions that are not resolved by lubrication, inspect the gas tube for obstruction.

  If cleaning is needed, a specialised gas tube brush (a thin, long flexible brush) can be run through the tube. A pipe cleaner with solvent works as a field-expedient alternative.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Gas Block">
  The gas block sits over the barrel at the gas port and directs gas into the gas tube. On most rifles, the gas block requires no regular maintenance beyond wiping the exterior clean. Adjustable gas blocks have a set screw or adjustment dial that should be checked periodically for security.

  If the gas port in the barrel becomes fouled (extremely rare in well-maintained rifles), symptoms include failure to cycle or sluggish bolt movement. A pick or dental tool can clear minor obstruction, but persistent gas port fouling warrants professional inspection.
</Accordion>

## Lubrication Points

Proper lubrication is what separates a rifle that runs reliably from one that malfunctions unpredictably. Apply lubricant to the following areas.

**Bolt Carrier Group** — Apply a light, even coat of oil to the exterior of the BCG, the bolt itself, and inside the carrier tube. A shiny coat is sufficient. Pay particular attention to the cam pin and the bolt's gas rings.

**Charging Handle** — Apply a small amount of oil to the channel where the charging handle rides inside the upper receiver, and to the latch mechanism.

**Pivot and Takedown Pins** — A single drop of oil on each pin keeps them moving freely and prevents them from seizing from corrosion over time.

**Inside the Upper Receiver** — The rails that the BCG rides on accumulate friction. Wipe them with a lightly oiled cloth.

**Lower Receiver** — The buffer tube interior and buffer spring benefit from a light coat of oil, particularly in humid environments.

<Tip>
  Use a quality CLP (Cleaner, Lubricant, Protectant) product that performs all three functions in one. Products like Break-Free CLP, Slip 2000 EWL, or Ballistol allow you to clean and lubricate in a single step during field conditions, reducing the time and number of products required. For deep cleaning sessions, a dedicated solvent (Hoppe's No. 9, M-Pro 7) outperforms CLP for removing heavy carbon — but always follow up with a dedicated lubricant rather than relying on the CLP's lubrication properties alone in those cases.
</Tip>

## Bolt-Action Maintenance

Bolt-action rifles are mechanically simpler than semi-automatics and require less frequent deep cleaning, but the same care and attention apply.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Bolt Cleaning">
    Remove the bolt from the receiver by pressing the bolt stop (location varies by manufacturer) and rotating the bolt handle upward, then pulling it rearward.

    **Cleaning the bolt**
    - Use a toothbrush and solvent to scrub the bolt face — the recessed area where the cartridge head seats. Carbon and primer residue accumulate here and can eventually affect headspace
    - Clean the extractor claw and the area beneath it; a failure to extract is often caused by a fouled extractor
    - Wipe the bolt body clean with a solvent-soaked cloth, then dry it
    - Inspect the firing pin protrusion — it should extend cleanly through the firing pin hole without hesitation. If the firing pin feels sluggish, disassemble per your manual and clean the firing pin channel

    **Lubricating the bolt**
    Apply a light coat of oil to the bolt body and cocking cam surface. Avoid over-lubricating — oil that migrates into the firing pin channel can slow the firing pin enough to cause misfires, particularly in cold temperatures.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Action Cleaning">
    Clean the receiver interior with a nylon brush and solvent. Pay attention to the locking lug recesses — the areas where the bolt's locking lugs seat into the receiver. These surfaces must be clean for the bolt to close fully and lock securely.

    Wipe the action dry and apply a very light coat of oil to the bolt raceways. In hunting and field use, avoid heavy lubrication that can attract debris and freeze in cold weather. Many experienced hunters run their bolt-action actions dry or with only a trace of dry lubricant (such as graphite or dry PTFE spray) in cold or dusty conditions.

    Inspect the trigger mechanism for debris, particularly if you have been shooting in dusty or wet conditions. Do not attempt to adjust trigger pull weight without proper training and tools — an improperly adjusted trigger is dangerous.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Barrel Cleaning">
    Bolt-action barrel cleaning follows the same procedure as described in the Cleaning the Bore section, with one important consideration: always clean from the chamber end toward the muzzle to preserve the muzzle crown.

    If your bolt-action rifle has a detachable box magazine, remove it and clean the magazine body and feed lips with a brush and cloth. If it has an internal magazine, clean the feed ramp area accessible from the action with a toothbrush.

    For rifles used for hunting in wet or salt-air environments, run a lightly oiled patch through the bore at the end of each day afield, even if you have not fired the rifle. This prevents rust in the rifling from moisture intrusion. Wipe the exterior metalwork with a lightly oiled cloth as well.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Maintenance Schedule

| Interval | Tasks |
|---|---|
| **After each range session** | Clean the bore with a pull-through or full rod-and-brush procedure; wipe down the BCG exterior; wipe the chamber; re-lubricate the BCG and bore; function check |
| **Every 500 rounds** | Full field strip and deep clean of BCG (including disassembly of bolt); scrub the chamber and feed ramp; inspect gas tube and gas block; inspect and replace worn gas rings on the bolt if needed; check extractor spring tension; wipe down all receiver surfaces |
| **Annually** | Professional inspection by a qualified gunsmith; headspace check (especially after high-round-count years); barrel throat erosion check; recoil buffer and spring replacement if heavily used; inspect all pins and fasteners for tightness; test function with defensive or hunting ammunition before relying on the rifle |
